How to Crate Train a Puppy: 7-Day Plan and Night Guide

Crate training turns on three skills that fail separately: settling, bladder control and being alone. Here is the plan with numbers \u2014 how long a puppy can hold it at each age, and a seven-day door schedule.

What crate training actually trains

Crate training is not really about the crate. It trains three separate things at once, and a puppy can be fine at one and hopeless at the next:

  • Settling — the ability to switch off in a small space while nothing is happening, which is the skill a dog needs on a vet table, in a car, or in a hotel room.
  • Bladder control — a puppy that will not soil its bed will hold on slightly longer each week, which is why house training goes faster with a crate than without one.
  • Being alone — the crate is where most dogs first practice being out of sight of their people, in short doses that do not tip into panic.

Three different skills means three different timelines. A puppy that sleeps quietly all night may still scream the moment you leave the room at 10am, and the fix for the second is not more time in the crate at night.

The finish line is a dog that walks into the crate on its own and lies down before you close the door. If you are still luring it in at week six, the pace was too fast somewhere.

Size it by measurement, not by weight

Almost every crate chart is organised by the dog's weight, and almost every one is wrong for somebody: a 12 kg dachshund and a 12 kg whippet need completely different boxes. Measure the dog instead. Two numbers decide the crate:

DimensionHow to measureCrate should be
LengthNose to tail base, standingThat figure plus 5–10 cm
HeightFloor to top of head, standingThat figure plus 5–10 cm
WidthTwice the shoulder widthEnough to lie flat on one side

The height figure is the one people get wrong: the dog has to be able to stand without crouching, sit without the top of its head touching the roof, and turn around in one movement. If any of those three is tight, the crate is too small, whatever the label says.

Too small is obvious. Too large is the mistake nobody warns about: a puppy with room to spare will pick a corner as a bathroom and sleep at the other end, which removes the one natural advantage a crate gives you in house training. That is what the divider is for.

The divider is the whole trick

Buy the crate the adult dog will need, then use the divider panel to shrink the usable floor to puppy size and move it back as the dog grows. Done this way, one crate covers the whole life of a medium dog:

AgeFloor space in useDivider position
8–10 weeksEnough to stand, turn and lie flat — often a third of the crateFront third
4–5 monthsHalfMiddle
7–9 monthsTwo-thirds, or the whole crate for a large breedBack panel removed

Skip the divider and you buy twice — a small crate the puppy outgrows in ten weeks, then the real one. If you are still choosing the box itself, sizing by weight and the latch designs that survive an escape artist are covered in our dog crate guide; this page assumes the crate is already right.

How long a puppy can actually hold it

The famous rule is “months of age plus one, in hours” — a three-month puppy holds it for four hours. It is a decent rule and it is routinely misused, because it describes a daytime maximum for a puppy that is awake and active, not a night-time target and not a schedule to aim for.

AgeDaytime, one stretchNight-time, one stretchWhat is normal at night
8–10 weeks1–2 hours3–4 hours1–2 wake-ups; a 3am trip is expected
3 months2–3 hours4–5 hoursUsually one wake-up
4 months3–4 hours5–6 hoursOften sleeps through, occasionally not
5–6 months4–5 hours6–8 hoursShould sleep through
Adult4–6 hours by choice; 8 at a stretch if needed8–10 hoursSleeps through

Two things push a puppy below its row on that table: drinking a lot in the hour before bed, and a growth spurt, which temporarily shortens the interval by roughly a week's worth of progress. Two things push it above: a puppy that is asleep rather than restless, and a crate small enough that it will not soil the floor.

Small breeds and toy breeds mature their bladder control later than the table suggests — expect the 3-month row to apply at 4 months. Large and giant breeds are the reverse.

The first seven days

The mistake is to treat week one as “getting the puppy used to the closed door”. The door is the last variable. Duration and distance are the two you turn, one at a time, and never both in the same session:

DayDoorWhere you areSession length
1Open, proppedSitting beside itMeals and chews only; no closing
2Closed, not latchedSitting beside it, hand on the door5–10 seconds, 5–6 times
3LatchedSitting beside it30 seconds, then 1 minute
4LatchedStanding, then stepping to the doorway2 minutes, three times
5LatchedIn the same room, doing something else5 minutes
6LatchedOut of the room for 30–60 seconds, back before any protest10–15 minutes total
7LatchedElsewhere in the house20–30 minutes

If a session ends in protest, the next one is shorter, not the same length repeated. Returning to the previous day is not a setback — it is the only way the puppy's experience stays “the door closes and nothing bad happens”.

The first night, hour by hour

The first night is its own event, and it fails for predictable reasons. The two that matter most are where the crate is and when the last meal was.

  • Last meal three hours before bed, water available until an hour before. A full bladder at 11pm is the single most common cause of a first night that goes badly.
  • Crate beside your bed for the first three to five nights. Not forever, and not in the room the dog will eventually sleep in. A puppy that can hear you breathing settles in minutes instead of an hour; the move to the hallway happens once nights are quiet, one room at a time.
  • Last toilet trip immediately before the door closes, on lead, boring, no play.
  • When it wakes at 3am — carry it out if it is small enough, keep the lights low, no talking, no play, toilet, straight back in. A puppy that gets a game at 3am will wake at 3am for months.
  • Do not open the door while it is crying. Wait for the half-second pause between cries, then open. Opening during a cry teaches that crying is the doorbell; opening in the pause teaches that quiet is.

Three cries, three different answers

“Ignore the crying” is standard advice and it is wrong roughly a third of the time. The sound tells you which of three things is happening:

SoundWhat it meansResponse
Rhythmic, steady, starts within two minutes of the door closing, pauses when you speakProtest. The puppy is objecting to the door, not suffering.Wait it out, then open in a pause. This is the only one to ignore.
Sudden, urgent, often with circling, scratching at the floor, or a change in postureNeeds to toilet nowOut immediately. This is not the cry to train against.
Escalating for more than ten minutes, panting, drooling, digging at the bars, trying to bite the latchPanic, not protestStop. Go back two days in the plan. A dog in this state is not learning to be alone, it is learning that confinement is terrifying.

The third row is the one that separates crate training from cry-it-out. A puppy that panics needs a slower plan, and if it happens every time, company while settling, a shorter duration, or a pen instead of a crate.

How much is too much

A crate is a management tool, and the limits are about welfare rather than training:

  • Puppies: no single stretch longer than the table above, and no more than three to four hours of crated time in a day outside sleep.
  • Adult dogs: a normal working day — up to eight hours — is the ceiling, and only if the dog gets a proper walk before and after. Nine or ten hours a day, five days a week, is not crate training, it is confinement with a routine.
  • Beyond that: a pen or a small room is the honest tool. It gives the dog room to move and still keeps the house safe.

Three things that go wrong quietly

None of these are training problems, which is why they get missed for weeks:

  • A collar left on in the crate. Tags and buckles catch in bar gaps, and a dog that panics while caught can injure its neck. Collar off before the door closes, every time.
  • A blanket over the whole crate. Covering three sides helps; covering the front as well, in a warm room, turns the crate into an oven. Leave the door end open, and check the temperature inside with your hand after twenty minutes.
  • The crate by a radiator, window or exterior wall. Puppies cannot regulate heat well. A spot that is comfortable to you in a t-shirt is usually right; a spot in direct sun through glass is not.

When the crate is the wrong tool

Two cases where a crate makes the problem worse rather than better:

Separation anxiety. The signature is damage concentrated at the exit — door frames, window sills, the crate latch itself — starting within thirty minutes of being left, with drooling and pacing. That is not a dog that needs more crate time. Confinement raises the panic, and the plan is a behavioural one, usually with a vet or a qualified behaviourist.

An older dog that has never been crated. A ten-year-old dog with no crate history and a sudden need for confinement is better served by a pen, a baby gate or a dog-proofed room. Crate training works on the first weeks of a habit, not on a decade of the opposite.

Bottom line

Buy the crate the adult dog needs, shrink it with the divider, and turn duration and distance one at a time. Meals go inside from day one, the door is the last thing you add, and the first three nights happen beside your bed. Most puppies are sleeping through within two to three weeks — and if the crying escalates rather than settles, the answer is a slower plan, not a longer one.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to crate train a puppy?
Most puppies accept the crate within a week and sleep through the night within two to three weeks. An older rescue dog can take longer if it has negative associations to confine.
Should I cover the crate at night?
Covering the top and sides helps many dogs feel secure, but leave the door area open for airflow. Stop covering if your dog gets anxious or overheats.
Is it cruel to crate a dog?
No — when introduced positively and not overused. A crate should be a voluntary safe space, not a place a dog is locked for eight hours a day.
How long can a puppy stay in a crate at night?
Three to four hours at 8-10 weeks, four to five at three months, five to six at four months, and a full night by five to six months. Toy breeds run roughly a month behind those figures. The daytime rule of months-plus-one is a ceiling for an awake puppy, not a night-time target.
Should the crate be in my bedroom?
For the first three to five nights, yes — beside the bed, where the puppy can hear you. A puppy that can hear you settles in minutes rather than an hour. Move the crate one room at a time once the nights are quiet.
What if my puppy pees in the crate?
First check the size: if there is room to sleep at one end and toilet at the other, the space is too large and the divider needs to move forward. Then check the interval — a puppy that soils after 90 minutes is telling you its limit is 90 minutes, not two hours. Never scold; clean with an enzymatic product so the spot stops smelling like a toilet.
Can I crate a dog while I am at work?
An adult dog can manage a normal working day of up to eight hours if it gets a walk before and after, but not as a five-day habit stacked on top of no other exercise. For a puppy, no: a four-month-old cannot hold it for a working day, so the options are a mid-day visit, a pen, or a dog-proofed room.
Will I need a bigger crate as the puppy grows?
Not if you buy the adult size and use the divider panel. Shrink the usable floor to puppy size at the front, move the divider back at four to five months, and remove it at seven to nine months for a medium dog.
My puppy screams in the crate — is that normal?
Screaming that escalates for more than ten minutes, with panting, drooling or digging at the bars, is panic rather than protest, and the answer is to go back two days in the plan rather than to wait it out. Steady rhythmic crying that pauses when you speak is protest, and that is the one to sit through.
