How to Leash Train a Dog — Loose Lead in Six Weeks

A dog pulls because pulling works. The fix is not a firmer correction — it is making a loose lead the only way forward.

Why dogs pull, in one sentence

A dog pulls because pulling gets it closer to what it wants. Every time a taut lead is rewarded with forward movement, the behaviour is reinforced — and most dogs are reinforced hundreds of times per walk.

This is the whole mechanism, and it explains why a firmer lead, a louder correction, or a different collar does not fix it. Those add discomfort, but the dog still moves forward while pulling, so the lesson is unchanged.

Before you train anything: spend the energy

A dog that has been in the house for ten hours has a full tank. Expecting a trained walk from an untrained, over-aroused dog is setting both of you up to fail.

Fifteen minutes of fetch, a sniff-heavy decompression walk on a long line, or a food-scatter in the garden before the training walk changes the whole session. This is not a trick — a dog below its arousal threshold can learn; one above it cannot.

The evidence on aversive collars is worth knowing: studies find higher rates of stress signals and increased aggression in dogs trained with them, and the behavioural suppression they produce often reappears as reactivity later. A harness and a bag of high-value treats will get further.

The core method: stop and turn

This is the technique that works on almost every dog, and it is almost aggressively simple.

1. Start walking. Lead loose, treat in hand at your side.

2. The moment the lead goes taut, stop. Not when the dog has been pulling for ten metres — the instant tension appears. Stand still and wait.

3. Wait for the dog to look back or step toward you. Any release of tension is the signal. Do not reel the dog in.

4. Mark and reward. Say "yes" the moment the lead slackens, then treat at your leg — the position you want the dog to occupy.

5. Walk on. Forward movement is the reward too, and it is the one the dog actually wants.

6. If the dog pulls again in three steps, stop again. In the first sessions you may cover twenty metres in ten minutes. That is normal, and it is the fastest route to a dog that walks properly.

Add the turn

For a dog that leans into the lead and will not stop pulling, add a direction change: the moment the lead tightens, say "this way" in a bright voice, turn 180 degrees and walk off in the other direction. The dog learns that tension means the walk changes direction, and that paying attention to you is what keeps things moving.

Do not yank. The turn is the consequence; the lead should never jerk the dog's neck.

Reward the position, not just the behaviour

The most common mistake in loose-lead training is rewarding at the wrong place. If you call the dog to you and treat in front of your body, the dog learns to walk in front and look back. If you treat at your left leg, the dog learns that the left leg is where good things happen.

Keep a handful of treats in the hand nearest the dog's reward position, and deliver low, at leg height, while you are still moving. Standing still to deliver every treat teaches the dog to stop.

Move to the next week only when the current one is reliable. A dog that fails week 3 three times in a row is not ready for week 4 — go back a step and shorten the sessions.

The distraction ladder

Dogs do not generalise. A dog that walks perfectly in the kitchen has learned "kitchen", not "loose lead". Work up this ladder one rung at a time, and expect to repeat the process at each level:

Kitchen → hallway → garden → quiet street → street with parked cars → street with people → street with dogs → park → park with squirrels. If the dog fails, you are one rung too high — drop back and build again.

Handling the determined puller

Use a front-clip harness as a temporary aid. It redirects forward momentum sideways and reduces the leverage a dog can generate, which makes the training physically possible for a small owner with a large dog. It is a tool for the training period, not a permanent solution.

Teach a "find it" cue. Scatter a few treats on the ground when the dog is about to lunge. It breaks the fixed stare that precedes most pulling, and it gives you a second to reposition.

Avoid the trigger, do not confront it. A dog that pulls toward other dogs needs distance, not a shorter lead. Standing at the edge of its threshold and feeding while it watches is the standard desensitisation approach — but at a distance where the dog can still eat.

What does not work

"He'll stop if I just keep walking." Dragging a dog on a taut lead reinforces pulling, because the dog is still moving forward.

Correcting with the lead. A pop on the lead suppresses the behaviour in that moment and teaches the dog that other dogs and people predict discomfort — one of the more reliable routes to reactivity.

Practising only on real walks. Real walks are the exam, not the lesson. The lesson happens in three-minute sessions in low-distraction places.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to leash train a dog?
With daily short sessions, most dogs walk reasonably on a loose lead in four to six weeks. A dog with a long history of pulling, or a highly distractible breed, takes longer.
Why does my dog pull on the lead?
Because pulling works — it moves the dog closer to what it wants. Every step taken while the lead is tight reinforces the behaviour.
Should I use a choke or prong collar?
No. Studies link aversive collars to increased stress and aggression, and they suppress behaviour rather than teach a loose lead. A harness and reward-based training works better.
What is the best harness for a dog that pulls?
A Y-shaped harness for comfort, or a front-clip harness as a temporary training aid that redirects forward momentum sideways.
How do I stop my dog pulling towards other dogs?
Increase distance until the dog can still take a treat and respond, then reward calm behaviour while other dogs are visible. Never shorten the lead and push forward.
Should I use a retractable lead for training?
No. A retractable lead keeps constant tension on the dog, which teaches pulling, and the thin cord causes serious injuries.
My dog walks well at home but pulls outside. Why?
Dogs do not generalise. A behaviour learned in the kitchen has to be retaught in the garden, on the street, and near other dogs — one level of distraction at a time.
How long should training sessions be?
Three to five minutes, three times a day. Short frequent sessions outperform one long one, and they end before the dog loses focus.
