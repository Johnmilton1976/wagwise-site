Why Do Dogs Eat Grass? The Evidence, Not the Myth

Most dogs that eat grass are perfectly healthy, and most do not vomit afterwards. The stomach-upset theory is the popular one and the weakest one.

The popular explanation does not hold up

Almost every owner has been told that dogs eat grass because their stomach is upset. It is a tidy theory, and the evidence does not support it.

A survey of more than 1,500 dog owners found that grass eating was reported in a large majority of dogs, that only a small minority showed any sign of illness before eating grass, and that most dogs did not vomit afterwards. If grass eating were a response to nausea, you would expect the opposite pattern: sick dogs, frequently.

What the data actually supports is simpler — grass eating is normal canine behaviour, it is common in well dogs, and in most cases it means nothing at all.

Why dogs might eat grass

No single explanation has been proven. The candidates, in rough order of how much support each has:

ExplanationHow much evidenceComment
Normal foraging behaviour, inherited from wild canidsModerateWolves and wild canids eat grass; stomach content analysis shows it regularly
Dietary fiber — the dog is seeking roughageModeratePlausible, and a fiber-poor diet is worth reviewing if it is constant
Taste and texture — young spring grass is sweetReasonableExplains why some dogs are picky about which grass
Boredom or displacement behaviourReasonableCommon in dogs left in a yard for long periods
Nausea reliefWeakThe popular explanation, and the one the surveys argue against
Self-medication against intestinal wormsVery weakRepeated online; no meaningful evidence

The nausea idea is not completely impossible — grass can irritate the stomach lining, and some dogs do vomit after eating a lot of it. But that is grass causing the vomiting, not the dog eating grass because it feels sick.

When grass eating is actually a signal

It stops being normal when it comes with other signs. Worth a vet visit:

  • Eating grass and vomiting repeatedly — especially if the dog also seems uncomfortable or refuses food.
  • Eating grass plus a swollen or painful abdomen — possible bloat, which is an emergency.
  • Pica — eating soil, stones, plastic, fabric or wood alongside grass. This can indicate anaemia, a mineral deficiency, gastrointestinal disease or a behavioural problem, and it needs investigation.
  • Weight loss despite a good appetite — malabsorption or a parasitic burden.
  • Grass eating that starts suddenly in an adult dog — a change in established behaviour is always worth noting.

The real danger is not the grass — it is what is on it

This is the part that matters practically, and the part that most articles skip.

Herbicides and pesticides. A treated lawn is a low-dose toxin exposure every time the dog grazes. Weedkiller, fertiliser granules and insecticide are all worth keeping a dog off for the interval stated on the label — usually longer than people wait.

Slug pellets (metaldehyde). Extremely attractive to dogs, extremely toxic, and scattered at ground level exactly where a grass-eating dog's nose goes. Tremors, high temperature and seizures follow quickly. This is one of the more common serious poisonings in garden dogs, and it is entirely preventable by not using the product.

Foxtail grass. In summer, the seed heads of foxtail and similar grasses harden into barbed awns that work their way into ears, noses, eyes, paw pads and even the skin between toes — and they only travel inward, because of the barbs. A dog that suddenly shakes its head, sneezes violently, or licks obsessively at a paw after walking in long grass may have a foxtail. Removal often needs sedation. This is a genuine seasonal emergency and the reason to keep dogs out of long dry grass in late summer.

Ticks. Long grass is where they wait. A grass-eating dog puts its face directly into the habitat.

What to do about it

If the dog is healthy and eats a bit of grass: nothing. Really — nothing. It is not a problem to solve.

If it is constant: review the diet for fiber content, and consider whether the dog is bored. A higher-fiber food or a vegetable addition to the bowl is a cheap trial. Increase exercise and enrichment if the dog spends long stretches alone in a yard.

If you want to offer grass safely: grow a tray of oat grass or wheatgrass indoors, or buy cat grass. It is cheap, it is clean, and it removes the pesticide and foxtail variables entirely. Many dogs that graze outdoors will happily use an indoor tray.

If the dog vomits after every grass session: that is worth a vet visit, because a dog that is repeatedly irritating its stomach is either eating too much of it or has an underlying stomach issue.

The short version

Grass eating in a well dog is not a symptom, not a mystery, and not something to treat. The stomach-upset theory is the one everybody repeats and the one the evidence least supports. Keep the dog off treated lawns and out of long dry grass in late summer, offer a clean indoor tray if you want to indulge it, and spend your worry on the things that are actually dangerous.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my dog eat grass?
Most likely normal foraging behaviour, possibly seeking fiber or because young grass tastes sweet. The popular 'upset stomach' explanation is not supported by the survey evidence — most grass-eating dogs are healthy and do not vomit.
Does eating grass mean my dog is sick?
Usually not. Most dogs that eat grass show no other signs of illness. It becomes a concern if it comes with repeated vomiting, a painful abdomen, weight loss, or eating non-food items like stones and plastic.
Why does my dog vomit after eating grass?
Grass irritates the stomach lining, especially in quantity. That means the grass caused the vomiting, not that the dog ate grass because it felt sick.
Is it safe for dogs to eat grass?
The grass itself is harmless. The risks are herbicides, pesticides, slug pellets, ticks and foxtail awns, all of which are on the grass rather than in it.
What is a foxtail and why is it dangerous?
A grass seed head with barbed awns that only travel inward. They lodge in ears, noses, eyes and paws and often need sedation to remove. Keep dogs out of long dry grass in late summer.
Can I grow grass for my dog indoors?
Yes — oat grass or wheatgrass trays are cheap and remove the pesticide and foxtail risks entirely. Many dogs use an indoor tray readily.
Should I stop my dog eating grass?
Not if the dog is healthy. If it is constant, review dietary fiber and check for boredom. Keep the dog off treated lawns and away from slug pellets.
Is grass eating a sign of worms?
There is no meaningful evidence for it, despite the claim being repeated widely. If worms are a concern, a faecal test is the way to find out.
