Dog Health & Symptoms — What to Watch For

Common dog health symptoms explained: vomiting, ear infections, not eating, shaking, and odd behaviors like eating grass or poop — plus when to call the vet.

Three questions that sort any symptom

Owners usually want a diagnosis. What they actually need at 11pm is a triage decision, and that comes from three questions: is the dog breathing normally, is it responsive and steady on its feet, and is there blood or a distended belly. If any answer is wrong, it is a now problem regardless of what else is going on.

What you are seeingHow fast to act
Difficulty breathing, blue or pale gums, collapse, seizure, bloated and unproductive retchingEmergency now — call the vet while you are on the way
Repeated vomiting, blood in stool or vomit, straining to urinate, eye injury, suspected poisoningToday — same-day appointment
Soft stool for a day, mild limping, occasional reverse sneeze, one episode of vomiting with normal energy afterWatch 24–48 hours, with a note of what changed

The distinction that matters most is energy. A dog that vomited once and then asked for a walk is in a different category from a dog that vomited once and went to lie in a dark room. Behaviour is the signal; the symptom count is not.

Normal values worth knowing by heart

MeasureNormal range for an adult dog
Body temperature38.0–39.2 °C (101–102.5 °F)
Resting breathing rate10–30 breaths per minute
Heart rateSmall breeds 120–160, medium 70–120, large 60–100 bpm
Capillary refill (gum press)Colour returns within 2 seconds

These numbers are less useful as absolutes than as your dog's own baseline. A breathing rate that has gone from 16 to 34 at rest is worth a call even though both ends sit inside the published range. Take the readings when the dog is calm and write them down — a number you can compare against is the whole point.

The combinations that are red flags

Single symptoms are usually minor. Combinations are what veterinarians react to. Vomiting plus a distended abdomen plus unproductive retching is a gastric dilation pattern and is fatal within hours without surgery. Drinking a lot plus urinating a lot plus weight loss points at diabetes or kidney disease rather than a behaviour problem. A head tilt plus flicking at one ear plus loss of balance is more than an ear infection. Pale gums plus weakness plus a recent injury is internal bleeding until proven otherwise.

If two or more of these appear together, do not wait for the third.

How to describe a symptom so the vet can help

Photograph it. A stool, a limp, a lump, a pile of vomit — a phone photo taken in the moment is worth more than any description you will manage on the phone an hour later. Then note four things: when it started, whether it is getting better or worse, what the dog ate in the previous 24 hours, and whether energy and appetite have changed.

That last pair is what separates "bring it in" from "watch it". Appetite and energy are the two most sensitive indicators a dog has, and they are the two owners are best placed to judge.
