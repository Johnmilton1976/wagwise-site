A starting point, not a prescription. Weigh every two weeks and adjust by about 10% against body condition — the number on this page does not know your dog's frame, and it cannot see the ribs.

What this calculator tells you that a bag chart does not

Two things, and they are the reason it exists.

It gives a range. Merck's own guidance is that any given animal may require as much as 30% more or less than the calories calculated for its body weight than another animal of the same weight. A calculator that prints one number and stops is quoting a population average as if it were your dog. This one prints the number, the band it sits in, and expects the band to be the part you actually use: start at the middle, weigh in a fortnight, move towards whichever edge the ribs point to.

It refuses to give you cups. Cups are the question every owner asks and the unit that cannot be answered across foods. Calorie density varies by more than 2× between diets, and studies of owners using measuring cups have found filling errors of 20–80% depending on the shape of the kibble and how the cup is packed. Grams and a cheap kitchen scale remove an entire category of overfeeding error. If a label states grams per cup, use it — but use it as a conversion for that one food, not as a universal answer.

How the number is worked out

Four steps, the same four the full feeding guide walks through in prose.

1. Resting energy requirement. RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)0.75. Energy scales with weight to the power of 0.75, not linearly, which is why a dog twice the weight does not need twice the food. Merck also gives a linear version for dogs over 2 kg — 30 × kg + 70 — which returns 370 kcal for a 10 kg dog against 394 from the exponential formula. The calculator uses the exponential one because it is the only one valid across the whole size range, including toy breeds and puppies.

2. Multiply by a life-stage factor. That is the second dropdown, and the numbers are Merck's, unmodified: intact adult 1.8, neutered adult 1.6, obesity-prone 1.4, puppies 3.0 under four months and 2.0 after. The result is the maintenance energy requirement.

3. Divide by the food. Grams per day = kcal needed ÷ (kcal per kg of food ÷ 1,000). Dry food is typically 3,500–4,500 kcal/kg; wet food is far lower because most of the tin is water, often around 1,000.

4. Check the dog, not the scale. Body condition score on a 1–9 scale, 4–5 ideal: ribs felt easily under light pressure and not seen, a visible waist from above, a tuck from the side. Recheck every two weeks. Drifting up the scale means cut the portion about 10% — not a third overnight, which produces a hungry dog and a plan nobody keeps.

Worked example, so you can check the arithmetic. A 10 kg neutered adult on a food stating 4,000 kcal/kg. RER = 70 × 100.75 = 394 kcal. Maintenance = 394 × 1.6 = 630 kcal. 630 ÷ 4,000 × 1,000 = 158 g a day, or 79 g a meal on two meals. The same dog on a wet food at 1,000 kcal/kg needs 630 g — the same calories, four times the weight of food.

Why the answer has a range in it

Individual variation in energy requirement is not noise around the formula; it is larger than most of the adjustments people make. Two Labradors of the same weight, same age, same neuter status, can differ by 30% in what they need to hold that weight. That is why every published factor is a starting point and why the only real measurement is the fortnightly weigh-in.

The practical version: feed the middle of the range for two weeks, weigh, and adjust by 10% at a time. If the dog is losing on a portion that should hold it, the portion is too small or something medical is happening — thyroid disease, pancreatic insufficiency, parasites and diabetes all present as "eats well, stays thin".

The treat budget sits inside the total, not on top of it

Treats are commonly held to about 10% of daily calories, with the rest of the diet completing the nutrition. That figure is a clinical rule of thumb rather than a published standard, and we say so rather than dressing it up as one. What matters is that it is a subtraction: the calculator prints the treat allowance next to the total, because "a few biscuits" on top of a full portion is how a diet that looks correct produces a dog that gains weight.

If you are using treats for training, take them out of the meal rather than adding them. The 47-food safety chart lists what is safe to use and, for each food, the number that decides whether it counts as a treat or a meal.

What this cannot do

It cannot score body condition, it does not know your dog's frame or muscle mass, and it does not adjust for medical conditions — kidney disease, heart disease, diabetes, pancreatitis and hypoallergenic elimination trials all change the target in ways a weight-based formula cannot see. Pregnant and lactating bitches need far more than any factor here. If the dog is on a prescription diet, use the amount the prescribing vet gave you.

Quick answers

How many grams a day should I feed my dog?

10 kg neutered adult on a 4,000 kcal/kg food: about 158 g a day, which is 79 g a meal on two meals. 20 kg on the same food: about 265 g. The formula is 70 × kg0.75 × 1.6, divided by the food's kcal/kg.

How many cups is that?

There is no conversion that holds across foods. Calorie density varies by more than 2×, and cup-filling error runs 20–80%. If your label states grams per cup, use that figure for that food only; otherwise weigh the food.

Why does the calculator show a range?

Because individual dogs of the same weight can need up to 30% more or less than the calculated figure. The single number is the middle of that band, not a target to hit exactly.

Should I use my dog's current weight or its target weight?

Target weight, for weight loss. Calculating a diet from the current weight over-feeds the dog you are trying to slim, and it is the most common reason a weight-loss plan stalls.

Does neutering change how much to feed?

Yes. The maintenance factor drops from 1.8 to 1.6 — roughly an 11% reduction in calories — while appetite typically does not fall at all, which is why weight gain in the year after the operation is so common.
