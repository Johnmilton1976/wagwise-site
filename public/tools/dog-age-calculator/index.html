How the estimate works

Dogs do not age at a flat 7× rate. The size-adjusted approximation we use treats the first year as about 15 human years and the second as roughly 9 more, then adds 4 to 6 per year afterward depending on size: smaller dogs age slower, larger dogs faster. That is why a 2-year-old small dog and a 2-year-old giant breed land in very different human-age ranges.

Why not just multiply by 7?

The 7× rule is usually traced to a piece of arithmetic rather than a study: a 70-year human life divided by a 10-year dog life. Both numbers have moved. A small dog now routinely reaches 14–16, and a human in a developed country can expect past 80, so even as a lifespan ratio the multiplier is closer to 5 for a small breed and 9 for a giant one — the two ends that the single number was supposed to average out.

Worse, it fails at the start. A one-year-old dog is already sexually mature, so 7× badly understates the early years and overstates the later ones. The size-adjusted curve — the one behind this calculator — bends by body size, because large and giant breeds age noticeably faster once they are adults. The shape of it is consistent with guidance from the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) and the Cornell Riney Canine Health Center.

A second formula, and why it disagrees

In 2020 a study comparing DNA methylation in dogs and humans produced a logarithmic formula rather than a multiplier. It fits the data better than any linear rule, and it is worth knowing because it produces very different numbers in the first two years:

The two converge from middle age and diverge sharply in youth. The reason is what each is measuring. The DNA clock describes biological ageing — how much molecular wear has accumulated — and a one-year-old dog has already packed a complete puberty, a full adult skeleton and most of its adult height into twelve months. The size-adjusted table describes life stage, which is what should change how you feed, train and insure the dog. A 1-year-old Great Dane is physically finished growing; a 1-year-old Chihuahua is not, and no single human-age number can carry both facts.

Both are estimates built on a Labrador-sized reference population, so neither is a measurement of your dog. The 2020 DNA-clock study used 104 Labrador Retrievers aged from puppyhood to 16 years, compared against 320 humans; the authors and the US National Institute on Aging both list testing the formula in other breeds as work still to be done. Applying it to a Chihuahua or a Great Dane is therefore an extrapolation, not a validated conversion — and those are the two breeds at opposite ends of the lifespan range. Use the DNA figure if the question is “how worn is this dog”; use the life-stage figure if the question is “what care does this dog need this year”.

Average lifespan, by size

The human-age conversion only makes sense against the lifespan it is compressing. These are the ranges vets and breed clubs generally quote:

SizeTypical lifespanSenior from
Toy and small (under 9 kg / 20 lb)12–16 years9–10
Medium (9–23 kg / 20–50 lb)10–14 years8
Large (23–40 kg / 50–90 lb)9–12 years7
Giant (over 40 kg / 90 lb)7–9 years6

The pattern is the opposite of the usual intuition: the bigger the dog, the shorter the life and the earlier old age starts. A Great Dane at seven is a senior; a Jack Russell at seven is still middle-aged. That is also why the same “human age” reading means different screening schedules — large breeds are typically watched for joint and heart disease years earlier than small ones.

The human-age output is really a stand-in for life stage, which is what should change how you care for the dog:

A "senior" reading is a useful prompt to book more frequent vet checks, watch for symptoms like vomiting or shaking, and adjust diet and exercise.

Breed-size reference

The size you pick maps roughly to these common breeds:

  • Small (under 9 kg / 20 lb): Chihuahua, Yorkshire Terrier, Dachshund, Shih Tzu, Jack Russell Terrier.
  • Medium (9–23 kg / 20–50 lb): Beagle, Border Collie, Cocker Spaniel, English Bulldog, Australian Shepherd.
  • Large (23 kg+ / 50 lb+): Labrador Retriever, German Shepherd, Golden Retriever, Rottweiler, Great Dane (at the giant end).

What the number can and cannot tell you

This tool gives a quick sense of your dog's life stage. It is an estimate, not a medical fact, and cannot tell you how healthy your dog is. Two dogs with the same human-age reading can be in very different shape. Use the number to time life-stage care — more frequent check-ups in the senior range, and earlier attention to joint support for large breeds — rather than to predict a lifespan.

When age matters most

As dogs move into the mature and senior ranges, a few things become worth watching: slower recovery after exercise, changes in appetite or water intake, and the onset of arthritis or dental disease. Our dog health guides explain what is urgent versus what can wait, and our care and grooming guides cover the at-home habits that keep older dogs comfortable.

Food questions owners ask next

Life stage changes what a dog can safely eat and how much. These are the guides owners open most after checking their dog's age: can dogs drink milk, can dogs eat chocolate, can dogs eat grapes, and how much to feed my dog. If the puppy-or-senior answer changed how much the bowl should hold, the dog food calculator takes the life stage as an input rather than as a paragraph.
