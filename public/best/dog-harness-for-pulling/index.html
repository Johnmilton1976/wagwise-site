Best Dog Harness for Pulling — Types, Sizing, Front vs Back

A front-clip harness turns a pulling dog back toward you, which gently discourages leaning in. We favor padded, adjustable, easy-on designs over trendy ones.

How these picks were made

No harness here was pull-tested to failure, and nobody measured gait changes with a force plate. What these picks rest on: strap geometry and how a front clip actually redirects load, the fit problems that consistently show up on dogs wearing harnesses badly (armpit rub, shoulder restriction, slipping out backwards), and the hardware details that fail first — buckles, D-rings and the stitching at the chest plate.

Six kinds of harness, and the one problem each solves

Almost every harness sold is one of six designs. The design decides what the harness can do; the brand decides how well it does it. If your dog pulls, only the second row matters, and the rest of this page is about getting that row right.

Dual-clipFront and backTraining now, a normal walk later — the same harness both waysCosts more, and the second ring is often the weak point on cheap versions

Watch for the seventh design. Some harnesses sold as “no-pull” work by tightening across the chest when the dog pulls. That is a choke collar distributed over a wider area — it stops the pull by making pulling uncomfortable, which is the mechanism the rest of this page argues against.

Why front-clip wins — mechanically, not as a slogan

A back-clip harness puts the attachment point above the shoulders. When the dog leans forward, the lead pulls up and back against the shoulders, and the dog's strongest muscles — the hindquarters driving forward — win. That is why a back-clip harness on a determined puller does nothing except make the pulling more comfortable for the dog.

A front clip puts the attachment point on the chest. Tension there turns the dog's front end sideways rather than stopping it. The dog's own forward momentum becomes the thing that redirects it, which is why the correction requires no force from the handler.

The often-quoted downside — that front-clip harnesses alter gait — comes from studies on dogs wearing weighted or restrictive harnesses, and the results vary by design. A well-fitted, Y-shaped front-clip harness that leaves the shoulder free is a different product from a strap that crosses the shoulder joint. Fit matters more than the category.

Fit matters more than brand. Two fingers should slide under every strap, and no strap should cross the shoulder joint or press into the armpit. A loose harness lets a strong dog back straight out of it.

The parts that fail first

Harness failures are not random. They happen at four points, in roughly this order, and each one is visible before you buy if you know where to look. A harness that fails at speed with a pulling dog is not an inconvenience — it is a dog loose near a road.

PartWhat goes wrongWhat to look for
Front D-ringPulls out of the webbing, or bends open under load; the single highest-load point on the harnessA welded metal ring attached through a box-X stitch, not a single line of stitching or a plastic ring
Side-release bucklesPop open when the dog hits the end of the lead, or when the chest strap is under tensionBuckles sized to the webbing, with a metal or heavy-duty acetal body; check them at the girth, which takes the most load
Chest-plate stitchingThe seam that joins the front panel to the girth strap lets go first, usually after months of washingBar-tacked or box-X at every junction, and webbing that continues past the seam rather than ending at it
Padding and edgesPadded panels that are only padded in the middle; bare webbing edges rub the armpit rawPadding that wraps the edge as well as the face, and a rolled or bound edge on any strap that touches skin
Adjustment slidersSlip under tension, so the harness loosens itself walk by walk until the dog can back outSliders with a locking bar or a double pass of webbing; test by pulling hard on each strap before the first walk

None of this is visible in a product photo, which is why the fit checks below matter more than the review section on any retail page. The one hardware detail worth paying for is the front ring: it is the part that carries the entire load of a dog that lunges, and it is the part cheapest harnesses save money on.

Price bands: what the money actually buys

Harnesses sell from about $15 to over $120, and the curve is not linear. Below a certain price you are buying webbing and hope; above a certain price you are buying colourways and a brand. Typical retail bands, roughly, and what changes as you move up:

The honest spending rule. Spend in the $25–50 band first. If the harness fits and the front ring holds, more money buys longevity, not better pull control. If it does not fit, no amount of money fixes it — a $120 harness on the wrong chest girth is a worse tool than a $30 one that fits.

One line runs through that table and it is worth stating plainly. The martingale-loop designs — Easy Walk, Freedom — tighten slightly across the chest as the dog pulls. They work, and they are not in the same category as a prong collar, but they do use a small amount of pressure as feedback. If you want pull reduction with no tightening at all, choose a fixed Y-front like the Front Range or the Balance; if you want the extra signal, the loop designs give it. Neither is cruelty; the choice is a preference about how much pressure you are willing to put on a dog's chest.

Sizing: measure the chest, not the dog

Harness sizes are not standardised. A medium from one maker overlaps a small from another, and the letter on the packet tells you nothing you can check. The only number that transfers between brands is the chest girth in centimetres, so measure it yourself and compare that to the maker's own chart.

MeasurementHow to take itWhy it is the one that matters
Chest girthStanding dog, tape around the widest part of the ribcage, just behind the front legs. Snug against the coat, not tightThis is the size the harness is built around. Everything else adjusts to it
NeckWhere a collar would sit, then add two fingers of roomDecides whether the harness goes over the head or steps in. Too tight here is what makes dogs refuse to be harnessed
Back lengthBase of the neck to base of the tailOnly needed for step-in harnesses and for coats worn under one

Weight is the input most sizing charts lead with, and it is the one that misleads. A 20 kg whippet and a 20 kg bulldog do not take the same harness — the whippet's chest is deep and narrow, the bulldog's is broad and shallow, and the difference in girth runs to double figures in centimetres. Two dogs of the same breed at the same weight can differ by a whole size depending on condition and coat.

Three rules that save a return. Measure a standing dog after a walk, not a sitting one — the tape reads smaller when the dog is folded up. Buy with 4–6 cm of adjustment left at the girth, because a winter coat adds girth without adding weight. And remeasure a puppy monthly: a harness bought at four months is a different size by seven.

The fitting check — five points

Put the harness on, then check each of these before the first walk:

1. Two-finger rule at every strap. Snug, not loose. A harness you can pull over the head without unbuckling is too big.

2. Shoulder clearance. Slide a finger under the front panel where it crosses the shoulder. It should move freely. If the strap sits on the point of the shoulder, the dog cannot extend the leg properly.

3. Armpit check. Walk the dog for two minutes, then look at the skin behind the front legs. Redness now means a rub that becomes a sore within a week.

4. Escape test. With the dog on a lead in a safe enclosed space, apply gentle backward pressure. A dog that can reverse out of the harness will do it at the worst possible moment, usually near traffic.

5. Lead attachment. Clip to the front ring for training, and check the ring is not pulling the chest strap sideways into the armpit when tension is applied.

Our picks by need

For a strong puller: a Y-front harness with a front clip and a back clip, padded at the chest plate. Use the front clip while training and switch to the back clip once the dog walks on a loose lead.

For a small dog or a puppy: the lightest front-clip harness available with four-point adjustment. Weight matters proportionally — a 300 g harness on a 4 kg dog is like a 5 kg backpack on an adult.

For a dog that slips harnesses: look for a harness with a third belly strap or a design that narrows behind the ribcage. These are harder to back out of, and they are the ones to choose for an escape artist.

For a short-coated or sensitive-skin dog: padded, smooth-edged webbing. Hairless and thin-coated breeds rub raw quickly on bare nylon.

What we avoid

Choke chains, prong collars and shock collars. This is not a style preference. Studies find higher rates of stress signals and increased aggression in dogs trained with aversive methods, and the suppression they produce often resurfaces later as reactivity toward the thing that predicted the correction. On a pulling dog, a prong collar teaches that other dogs predict neck pain.

No-pull harnesses that cinch the ribcage shut. A light martingale loop (the Easy Walk style) is a pressure cue; a design whose whole mechanism is closing a strap around the ribcage is a choke collar with wider distribution. The test is visual: if a strap visibly shortens on the dog's chest as it pulls, leave it on the shelf. A fixed Y-front does the same job without asking the dog to work against pressure.

Retractable leads for training. Constant tension teaches pulling, and the thin cord has caused severe injuries — including amputations — when it wraps around a limb or a person.

The gear is half the job

A front-clip harness makes pulling physically harder. It does not teach the dog what to do instead. The training that goes with it:

Stop the moment the lead goes taut. Not after ten metres — the instant tension appears. Wait for the dog to look back, then reward at your leg.

Reward the position, not the return. Treat low, at leg height, while you are moving. Treating in front of your body teaches the dog to walk ahead and look back.

Practise in three-minute sessions indoors. Real walks are the exam; the lesson happens in the kitchen. Full method: how to leash train a dog.

Bottom line

Pair a well-fitted, Y-front, padded front-clip harness with the stop-and-turn method. The gear buys you physical control while the training takes effect — usually four to six weeks of short daily sessions. Expect the harness to be a training aid you phase out of, not a permanent solution to a dog that has never been taught to walk on a loose lead.

Frequently asked questions

What type of harness stops pulling?
A front-clip (chest-clip) harness. It turns the dog sideways when they pull, so forward momentum redirects instead of being rewarded. A back-clip harness lets a strong dog pull like a sled dog.
Are no-pull harnesses bad for dogs?
Good padded Y-front front-clip harnesses are safe. Avoid designs that tighten and constrict the chest, which are aversive tools with the same problems as a choke collar.
Should I use a collar or harness?
For a puller, a front-clip harness protects the neck. Keep ID tags on a flat collar underneath — a harness is easy to slip if the dog gets loose.
Do front-clip harnesses change a dog's gait?
Studies on restrictive and weighted harnesses show gait changes; well-fitted Y-front designs that leave the shoulder free are a different product. Fit matters more than the category.
How tight should a dog harness be?
Two fingers should slide under every strap, and the front panel should not sit on the point of the shoulder. If you can pull it over the head without unbuckling, it is too big.
Why does my dog keep slipping out of the harness?
Usually too loose, or a design without a third belly strap. Check the escape test in a safe enclosed space — dogs that can reverse out will do it near traffic.
How do I measure my dog for a harness?
Measure the chest girth: a tape around the widest part of the ribcage, just behind the front legs, on a standing dog. Compare that number in centimetres to the maker's chart rather than buying by weight or by the letter on the packet, because S/M/L are not standardised between brands.
What is the difference between a front-clip and a back-clip harness?
A back-clip attaches the lead above the shoulders, which suits a dog that already walks well and does nothing about pulling. A front-clip attaches on the chest, so tension turns the dog sideways instead of letting it lean into the lead.
Can I leave a harness on all day?
It is better not to. Long-term wear causes rubbing at the armpits and pressure points. Put it on for walks and take it off indoors.
How much should I spend on a no-pull harness?
Plan for $25–50. That band buys the two things that matter: a metal front ring stitched through webbing, and four points of adjustment. Under $25 the front ring is usually plastic or single-stitched, which is the part that fails when a dog lunges. Above $50 you are mostly paying for durability and finish, not better pull control.
Do front-clip harnesses hurt a dog's shoulders?
Not a well-fitted Y-front one. The gait concerns in the literature come from restrictive or weighted harnesses, and from front panels that cross the point of the shoulder. A Y-shaped panel that leaves the shoulder free is a different product — check by sliding a finger under the front strap where it passes the shoulder; if it sits on the joint, the harness is the wrong shape for that dog.
