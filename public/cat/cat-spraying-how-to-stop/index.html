How to Stop a Cat Spraying Indoors

Spraying is your cat marking territory with urine on vertical surfaces — different from litter accidents. The fix is reducing stress and conflict, not punishment.

Spraying or urinating outside the box? They are different problems

Getting this distinction right decides whether you fix it in a week or fight it for a year. They look similar in a house and have almost nothing in common in cause.

Spraying (marking)Inappropriate urination
PostureStanding, tail up and often quivering, urine directed backwards at a vertical surfaceCrouching, normal squat, urine on a horizontal surface
VolumeSmall — a few millilitresNormal or large amount
HeightUsually 20–60 cm up a wall, door, curtain or chair backOn the floor, a bed, in the bath
Typical causeTerritorial stress — other cats, changes, conflictMedical (cystitis, stones, arthritis) or litter aversion

If it is crouching and producing a full bladder's worth, stop reading about marking and read litter box avoidance instead — and book a vet visit, because urinary disease in cats is common and painful.

Rule out medical causes first

Spraying is behavioural, but cats in pain also mark. Before treating this as a stress problem, have a vet check for:

  • Feline lower urinary tract disease (FLUTD) — cystitis is extremely common in cats and causes frequent, painful urination that can look like marking.
  • Bladder stones or crystals — a male cat with an obstruction is a genuine emergency and can die within 24–48 hours. Straining with no urine produced is an emergency, not a behaviour issue.
  • Hyperthyroidism and diabetes — both increase urination.
  • Arthritis — a cat that cannot comfortably get into the box may urinate somewhere easier to reach.
A male cat straining in the litter box with little or nothing coming out, crying, or licking at the genitals needs a vet now. That is a urethral obstruction, not marking, and it is fatal without treatment.

Why cats mark: the territorial math

Cats mark when they perceive that their territory is contested, unstable, or too small for the number of cats in it. The usual triggers, in rough order of frequency:

  • Other cats outdoors — visible through a window or glass door, or entering through a cat flap. This is the most under-diagnosed trigger: the cat is not in the house and the owner never sees it.
  • Too many cats in too little space, or a change in the group's dynamics.
  • New furniture, a new baby, a new partner, a renovation — anything that changes the smell of the core territory.
  • A move, especially in the first weeks when the cat has no established scent map.
  • Unneutered males, and females in season.

Neutering: the biggest single lever

Around 90% of entire male cats spray, and neutering reduces or eliminates it in the large majority — roughly 80–90% stop, with the best results when the operation happens before the habit is established. A male neutered after years of marking often reduces but does not fully stop, because the behaviour has become a learned routine on top of the hormonal drive.

Females spray far less often, but spaying removes the oestrus-driven marking entirely. If a cat in the house is entire, that is the first thing to change.

Cleaning: the part most people get wrong

Cat urine contains uric acid crystals that bind to porous surfaces and keep releasing scent for years. Ordinary household cleaner removes the smell for humans and leaves the marker intact for the cat — which is why the same corner gets marked again and again.

Use a biological enzyme cleaner, flood the area rather than wiping it, and let it air dry rather than towelling it off — the enzymes need contact time. For carpets, work it down to the underlay. If the spot keeps coming back, a UV torch in a dark room will find dried marks you never knew about, including on skirting boards and the backs of furniture.

Do not use ammonia-based cleaners. Urine contains ammonia, so an ammonia cleaner reads to the cat as "another cat's mark here", which invites overmarking. Do not use bleach either — the chlorine smell is unpleasant to cats and the mixture with urine is not something to create indoors.

Reducing the territorial pressure

Block the view of outdoor cats. Frosted film on the lower half of the window, or a curtain drawn to cat height. It sounds trivial; it is often the whole fix.

Stop cats entering. Lock or microchip the cat flap. If a neighbour's cat comes in to eat, that is a direct territorial invasion and the marking will continue until it stops.

Increase resources so nobody competes. The rule is one litter box per cat plus one, in separate locations; separate feeding stations; multiple water sources; and vertical space — shelving, cat trees, high resting spots — so a lower-ranked cat can get out of sight. Resource competition is a major driver of marking in multi-cat homes.

Use synthetic pheromones. A plug-in diffuser (Feliway Classic) in the main marked room reduces anxiety-driven marking in a meaningful share of households. It is not a cure alone, and it takes a few weeks. It is worth trying because it is cheap and harmless.

What not to do

Do not punish. Shouting, spraying water, or pushing the cat's nose in it does three things: it raises the anxiety that causes marking, it teaches the cat to mark where you cannot see, and it damages the relationship that makes the cat feel secure in the territory. All three make the problem worse.

Do not confine the cat indefinitely as a solution. Short-term confinement to a small, scent-familiar room can be part of a reset in a multi-cat conflict, but it is a technique with a plan and an end point, not a permanent answer.

Do not add another cat to "keep it company". It increases competition for territory and usually increases marking.

When to ask for medication

If marking continues after neutering, medical clearance, environmental change, pheromones and six to eight weeks of consistent work, a behaviourally trained vet can prescribe. Clomipramine and fluoxetine are the two most used, and both have reasonable evidence for reducing anxiety-driven marking in cats.

Medication is not a failure and it is not a sedative — it lowers the anxiety baseline enough for the environmental work to take effect. It is usually combined with behaviour modification, not used instead of it.

Frequently asked questions

Is cat spraying the same as peeing outside the box?
No. Spraying is a small amount on a vertical surface with the tail up, for marking. Accidents are a full-volume crouching urination on the floor, usually medical or litter-related.
Will neutering stop my cat spraying?
Usually — roughly 80–90% of males stop or greatly reduce, with the best results when neutered before the habit forms. An older male may reduce rather than stop completely.
How do I clean cat spray?
Use a biological enzyme cleaner, flood the area and let it air dry so the enzymes have contact time. Never use ammonia or bleach — ammonia smells like urine and invites remarking.
Why has my cat suddenly started spraying?
The most common triggers are another cat outside the window, a new cat or pet in the home, a move or renovation, and resource competition. Rule out urinary disease first.
Does spraying mean my cat is angry at me?
No. It is territorial communication driven by insecurity, not spite. Punishment increases anxiety and makes marking more likely, and it teaches the cat to hide it.
Do pheromone diffusers work for spraying?
They help in a meaningful share of cases by lowering anxiety, and they are cheap and harmless. They work best combined with environmental changes, not alone.
Can female cats spray?
Yes, though less often than males. Entire females spray around oestrus; spaying removes that driver.
When should I see a vet about spraying?
Immediately if the cat is straining to urinate with little output, especially a male — that is a possible obstruction and an emergency. Otherwise, see a vet early to rule out FLUTD before treating it as behaviour.
