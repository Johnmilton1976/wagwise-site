How to Stop a Cat Scratching Furniture

Scratching is normal cat behavior you cannot train away — but you can redirect it. Give appealing posts and make the furniture boring.

Why cats scratch, and why punishment never works

Scratching is not destruction and it is not spite. It does four things at once: it sheds the outer sheath of the claw, it stretches the muscles of the shoulders and back, it leaves visible marks that read as territory to other cats, and it deposits scent from glands between the paw pads.

That last point is why punishment backfires so reliably. A cat that is squirted or shouted at while scratching does not learn "don't scratch" — it learns "don't scratch while the human is watching", and it moves to a quieter corner of the same sofa. You lose the ability to see where the marking is happening, which makes the problem harder, not easier.

The three features that make a post get used

Most rejected scratching posts were rejected for measurable reasons. Get these three right and the odds improve immediately.

FeatureWhat worksWhat fails
HeightTall enough that the cat can stretch fully upright — at least 80 cm, more for a large catKnee-height posts the cat cannot extend on
StabilityBase heavy enough that it does not wobble or tip when pulledA post that rocks — cats will not commit weight to it
MaterialSisal rope wound around a post, or corrugated cardboardSmooth carpet that gives no resistance and shreds

The single most common mistake is buying a post that is too short. A cat wants to hang its full body weight off the front paws and stretch — a short post forces it into a crouch, which is not the movement it is trying to perform.

Vertical or horizontal?

Cats have a preference and they will tell you which. A cat that scratches the arm of the sofa, the door frame, or the corner of the wall wants vertical. A cat that scratches the carpet, a rug, or the top of a wooden floor wants horizontal — provide a cardboard scratcher laid flat.

Many cats want both, at different times. If you only offer vertical and the cat keeps going for the rug, you have offered the wrong thing, not a bad cat.

Where to put it

Position matters more than most people expect. Put a post:

  • Right next to the furniture being scratched — this is the single highest-value placement, because it competes directly with the existing habit.
  • Next to where the cat sleeps. Most scratching happens on waking, as a stretch. A post by the sleeping spot gets used several times a day for free.
  • Near the entrance to the room, and near the main path through the house.

One post in a spare room will not compete with a sofa in the living room. In a multi-cat household, one per room the cat spends time in is closer to right than one per house.

How to redirect, step by step

1. Make the post more interesting than the sofa. Rub catnip or silvervine on the sisal, or hang a toy from the top. Some cats ignore both; a piece of string dragged up the post works on nearly all of them.

2. Move the cat, don't move the post. When you catch the cat scratching furniture, pick it up calmly and place it on the post — front paws on the sisal. Do not scold. Praise and treat when it scratches the post on its own.

3. Make the furniture unrewarding. Double-sided tape on the scratched corner, a throw over the arm, or a sheet of aluminium foil. Cats dislike the feel and the noise. This is temporary — it buys time for the new habit to form, usually two to four weeks.

4. Do not remove the tape too early. The most common failure is taking the deterrent off after three days, at which point the cat returns to the sofa and the post goes back to being ignored.

Nail trims and soft caps

Trimming every two to three weeks reduces the damage each scratch does, even though it does nothing about the motivation. Cut only the clear tip, well in front of the pink quick — cutting into the quick is painful and makes the cat uncooperative for months. If you are unsure, ask a vet nurse to show you once.

Soft plastic caps glued over the claws are safe and effective for furniture protection, and they last four to six weeks. They must be replaced as the claw grows, and they do not remove the need to scratch — the post stays essential.

Why declawing is not an option

Declawing is not nail removal. It is amputation of the last bone of each toe — ten separate amputations, equivalent to removing the tips of a person's fingers at the last joint. Documented consequences include chronic pain, reluctance to use the litter box (because scratching in litter hurts), increased biting, and lifelong gait changes.

It is banned or restricted in much of Europe, in parts of Canada and Australia, and in several US states and cities. Every major veterinary behaviour body advises against it. If a landlord or a partner is pushing for it, the answer is a behaviour consultation, not surgery.

Special cases

Senior cats. Arthritis makes stretching on a tall post painful. Add a low, horizontal scratcher and a ramp to the favourite high spot. A cat that suddenly stops scratching and starts missing the litter box may be in pain, not badly behaved.

Kittens. Scratch drive is strongest during play and after sleep. Provide a post next to the play area and redirect every time — this is the easiest age at which to build the habit.

Multi-cat homes. Scratching is also communication between cats. A household with four cats needs several posts in separate locations, or the lowest-ranked cat will scratch where it feels safe and that is usually the back of a sofa.

Frequently asked questions

How do I stop my cat scratching the couch?
Put an appealing post right beside the couch, use catnip or a toy to draw attention to it, and make the couch unrewarding with double-sided tape or a cover for two to four weeks. Reward post use.
Should I declaw my cat?
No. Declawing amputates the last bone of each toe — ten amputations — and causes chronic pain and behaviour problems. It is banned in many countries. Redirect scratching instead.
Do claw caps work?
Yes. Soft caps protect furniture for four to six weeks and are safe. They do not remove the need to scratch, so keep the posts.
Why won't my cat use the scratching post?
Usually because it is too short, unstable, or made of smooth carpet. It needs to be tall enough for a full stretch, heavy enough not to wobble, and covered in sisal or cardboard.
How tall should a scratching post be?
At least 80 cm for an average cat, and taller for a large breed — the cat must be able to stretch fully upright with front paws extended.
Does punishing a cat for scratching work?
No. Punishment teaches the cat to scratch out of sight, which makes the problem invisible rather than solved, and damages trust.
How often should I trim my cat's claws?
Every two to three weeks. Cut only the clear tip in front of the pink quick — cutting into the quick is painful.
Do cats prefer vertical or horizontal scratchers?
It varies. A cat scratching the sofa arm or door frame wants vertical; a cat scratching the carpet wants horizontal. Many want both.
