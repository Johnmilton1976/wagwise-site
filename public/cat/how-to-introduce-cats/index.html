How to Introduce Cats — the Scent-First Plan That Works

Almost every cat fight between housemates traces back to the same mistake: face-to-face introductions on day one. Cats build relationships through smell first, and a staged plan that respects that takes weeks — and prevents years of friction.

Why scent comes before sight

A cat's social world runs through its nose. Cats read identity, mood and history in scent profiles — their own group scent, and the smell of strangers. Two cats meeting through a glass barrier with no scent exchange are, to each other, an alarm: unfamiliar smell, direct eye contact, no escape. That is why the universal failure mode is the same: carry the new cat into the living room and "let them sort it out". What they sort out is a hierarchy of fear, and it can last for years.

The plan below runs in stages. The timing rule that matters: move up a stage only when both cats are relaxed at the current one — eating normally, playing, not posted at the barrier. Days per stage is typical; some pairs take weeks. Going back a stage is not failure, it is the method.

Stage 1 — Base camp, and a normal resident cat

Set the newcomer up in one room with everything: litter tray (one per cat plus one, remember), food and water away from the tray, hiding places, a scratching surface, warmth. The new cat needs days to decompress; the resident cat keeps their normal routine and gets their usual access to the rest of the home. Do not visit the base camp with the resident cat in your arms — you are carrying one cat's smell into the other's territory, in the worst possible format.

Expect the newcomer to hide, eat little and be vocal for the first day or two. A cat that will not eat at all for more than about 24–48 hours needs a vet, not more time.

Stage 2 — Scent swap

Before any visual contact, trade smells daily: swap bedding between rooms, and use the sock trick — stroke a clean sock along the new cat's cheek (where the scent glands are) and leave it in the resident cat's area, then repeat in reverse. Feed each cat near their side of the door so the other's smell starts arriving in a good context: dinner. If either cat hisses flatly at the sock or refuses to eat near the door, stay here and keep swapping.

Stage 3 — Food is the progress gate

The honest readiness signal is appetite. Both cats eating calmly on opposite sides of the closed door — not gobbling in panic, not guarding the gap — means the smell of the other cat has started to mean "food happens" rather than "threat happens". That usually takes several days of stage 2. This is the gate to the next stage, and it cannot be rushed by wanting it to be over.

Stage 4 — Sight through a barrier

Open visual contact briefly, through a gap, a screen door, a stair gate or a door held ajar with a wedge — a barrier both cats can see through but not push past. Short sessions, a few minutes, ending while both are still calm, several times a day. Keep feeding on opposite sides of the barrier during this stage; the goal is that seeing the other cat predicts something good. Calm sniffing and looking away are wins. Fixated staring is not — end the session and close the door.

Stage 5 — Supervised shared time

Remove the barrier for short, supervised periods with a clear exit route for each cat. Parallel activities work best: playing with each cat separately on either side of the room, feeding bowls well apart, two litter trays in different locations. Keep the base camp room available to the newcomer for weeks — a retreat they control is what lets a nervous cat choose to come out.

Never pick up either cat during a face-off, and never force them closer "to say hello". A restrained cat is a cornered cat. If a meeting goes badly, separate calmly, give everyone an hour, and drop back one stage. Escalating a bad meeting is how one bad day becomes a permanent feud.

Reading the body language

  • Normal early days: hissing, growling, avoidance, one cat posted at a distance watching. Uncomfortable but functional — this is negotiation.
  • Handle it now: silent frozen staring, blocking doorways, chasing that ends in screaming or fur flying, one cat refusing to eat or stopping using a tray because the other controls it.
  • Go back a stage for anything in the second list, and add resources first: more trays, more feeding stations, more vertical space. Resource guarding, not personality, causes most multi-cat conflict — the setup is in our litter box guide, and the marking that sometimes follows a feud is covered in the spraying guide.

When to get help

If after a month of staged introductions there are still serious fights, or one cat has stopped eating, hiding permanently or soiling, stop self-managing and ask your vet for a referral to a qualified behaviourist. Persistent redirected aggression — a cat attacking a housemate after seeing a strange cat through a window — is a real clinical problem, not a training failure, and it responds much better to early professional help than to months of trial and error.

Frequently asked questions

How long should introducing two cats take?
Most pairs need two to four weeks to reach relaxed shared time; some need a couple of months. The clock that matters is the behaviour at each stage — eating calmly and no blocking or staring — not the calendar.
Is hissing normal when cats first meet?
Yes, through the barrier stages it is normal negotiation. Fixated silent staring, blocking doorways, or fights with screaming and fur are not — separate, then drop back one stage.
Should I just let them fight it out?
No. Cats do not settle hierarchy by fighting it out the way some animals do; unrehearsed conflict tends to create lasting avoidance and marking. Staged scent-first introductions prevent the fight instead of surviving it.
