Cat Not Using the Litter Box? Reasons First, Fixes Second

A cat that stops using the litter box is not being spiteful. Something about the box, the spot, or their body has stopped working for them — and the order you check those things decides how fast this gets fixed.

Rule out the vet before you change anything

The most common reason a previously reliable cat starts missing the box is physical, not behavioural. Urinary tract disease makes urination painful, and cats connect that pain to the place where it happened — the box. A cat with a burning bladder does not think "the litter hurt me"; they think that spot hurts, so they go somewhere else and often still squat in a box-like context: laundry, a bath mat, a bed.

  • Go same-day if: there is blood in the urine, frequent trips that produce only drops, crying in the tray, or — critical in male cats — straining with nothing coming out. A blocked urethra kills within a day or two; this is an emergency, not a behaviour issue.
  • Book a normal visit if: the change came with extra thirst, weight loss, accidents that are soft stool rather than urine, or the cat is over about ten years old — constipation, kidney disease, an overactive thyroid and arthritis all change toileting habits.
  • Arthritis is the quiet one. A senior cat with stiff hips will not leap into a high-sided tray they used easily at five. A tray with a cut-down entry often fixes an "attitude problem" that was actually a jump problem.

Soiling or marking? They are different problems

Before any setup work, work out which of the two you are dealing with, because the fixes barely overlap. Soiling is a full squat on a horizontal surface — a puddle on the floor, rug or bed. Marking (spraying) is a cat standing up, tail quivering, delivering a small streak at nose height on a wall or furniture. If you are seeing vertical streaks, that is territorial communication, not box rejection — the approach is different, and we cover it in the spraying guide. This page is about soiling.

The one-per-cat-plus-one rule — and why placement beats brand

The single most documented rule in the litter literature: one tray per cat, plus one extra, spaced around the house. In a two-cat home that means three, and putting all three side by side in one room does not count — to a cat that is one resource location another cat can guard. Corridors, corners and quiet landings beat next-to-the-washing-machine: cats avoid trays that can startle them with a spin cycle or trap them in a dead end.

Placement also has a hard rule most homes break: trays away from food and water, away from cat flaps and full-length glass (a cat feels watched from outside), and somewhere reachable without walking past the other cat's favourite ambush point.

The box maths most homes get wrong

  • Size: the tray should be about one and a half times the cat's body length. Many retail trays are simply too small for an adult cat to turn around and dig in.
  • Entry: low sides for kittens, seniors and arthritic cats; high-sided or top-entry trays suit the athletic and the enthusiastic digger only.
  • Hoods: many cats tolerate them, but a hood traps smell at nose height and blocks escape routes — in a multi-cat home an uncovered tray is the safer default.
  • Cleaning: scoop at least once, ideally twice a day; full wash every two to four weeks with clumping litter. Use hot water and plain detergent — some disinfectants (anything with chloroxylenol, e.g. Dettol-type products) are toxic to cats, and heavy perfume puts cats off.

Litter preference is real, not fussiness

Preference studies keep finding the same thing: most cats prefer fine-grained, clumping litter over coarse or pelleted material, and scented products lose to unscented. That is a tendency, not a law — the individual cat is the judge. The practical part: when you change litter, do it gradually (new litter sprinkled over the old, increasing over a week) or keep one tray on the old litter while the rest switch. An abrupt switch is a classic trigger for a cat that has used one product all its life.

The reset, step by step

  1. Clean every soiled spot with an enzymatic cleaner. Ammonia-based products smell like urine to a cat and can invite a repeat.
  2. Add a tray where the accidents happen, even if that spot is inconvenient. Once the cat is using it reliably, move it a few inches a day toward where you want it.
  3. Offer options for a while: two litter types in side-by-side trays and let the cat's usage answer the preference question.
  4. Never punish. Rubbing a cat's nose in it or confining them to a dirty tray does not teach box use — it teaches fear of you and adds stress, and stress is a known trigger for feline idiopathic cystitis, which brings you back to step one.

Most soiling cases that are behavioural resolve within one to two weeks of a correctly set up reset. If nothing changes after two honest weeks of this — with the vet visit done — ask your vet for a behaviour referral, because persistent cases usually have a specific trigger (another cat outside the window, a new baby, a house move) that needs targeted work.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my cat suddenly peeing on my bed or laundry?
Soft, absorbent items that smell like you are a common target when urination hurts or the box feels unsafe. Treat it as a symptom: vet check first, then review the box setup — and wash with enzymatic cleaner, not ammonia.
How many litter boxes should I have for two cats?
Three, in different locations. One per cat plus one spare is the rule, and the spare exists because in multi-cat homes one cat can block another's access to a single location.
Will a covered litter box fix the problem?
Usually not, and it can make things worse. Hoods trap odour at the cat's nose height and limit escape routes. Uncovered is the safer default, especially in multi-cat households.
How long until the litter box problem is fixed?
Medical cases improve when the illness is treated. Behavioural soiling usually responds within one to two weeks of a full reset; if it does not, get a behaviour referral rather than trying harder versions of the same thing.
