Why Are My Cats Fighting in the Same House?

Two cats who lived together peacefully rarely "hate" each other overnight. Most household fights trace back to three specific causes — and one of them, non-recognition aggression, almost never gets mentioned in the usual advice.

Fighting or playing? The table that settles it

Before anything else, be honest about what you are seeing. Play between cats is loud in movement and quiet in sound; fighting is the opposite. Watch for these five markers:

PlayFight
SoundMostly silent, some chirpingYowling, hissing, growling, screaming
ClawsSheathed — no fur fliesOut — you find tufts of fur, scratches
Turn-takingBoth cats chase and get chasedOne always chases, one always flees
Ears and tailForward or neutral, loose tailFlattened ears, puffed tail, crouched bodies
AfterwardsEat, sleep, groom togetherAvoid each other for hours or days; one hides

Fur tufts, scratch wounds, or a cat who now guards a room and refuses to eat near the other are not play injuries. If the fleeing cat is not eating, hiding all day, or eliminating outside the box since the fight started, treat it as conflict with real consequences — see why cats stop using the litter box.

The three causes almost every household fight traces to

1. Redirected aggression — the most common cause vets see. A cat sees or smells an intruder (a strange cat through the window, a neighbour's dog) and, unable to reach it, attacks the nearest target: the housemate. It looks like the cats turned on each other for no reason, because the actual trigger was invisible to you. This is why a fight that started near a window or right after you saw a stray outside is never a coincidence.

2. Non-recognition aggression — the one articles miss. A cat comes home from the vet, boarding, or grooming smelling different — clinic scents, disinfectant, stress hormones. The housemate does not recognise the smell and treats the returning cat as a stranger, and the fight can be serious. Every cat owner should know this exists, because the response is not training: separate the returning cat for a few hours, exchange bedding between the two cats, and re-introduce scent before full contact. One vet visit can start a feud that lasts months if this is handled with a shrug.

3. Resource tension. Cats in multi-cat homes compete over food stations, water, litter boxes, resting spots, and above all height. If there is one litter box in the basement and one food bowl in a corridor with two exits, one cat is being ambushed at its most vulnerable moments. The n+1 rule — one resource per cat, plus one extra, spread across rooms — is not a luxury, it is conflict prevention. The same tension shows up as spraying on vertical surfaces.

What to do in the first five minutes (and what never to do)

  • Never reach in with your hands. An aroused cat redirects onto whatever it touches — most fight injuries in humans happen during separation. Use a barrier: slide cardboard between them, drop a blanket over one, or clap and block rather than grab.
  • Separate fully for at least a few hours. One cat in a quiet room with food, water and a litter box. Adrenaline takes hours to clear, and re-contact too early restarts the fight.
  • Do not punish. Spraying water or yelling stops the fight in front of you and teaches both cats that the other cat predicts bad things — the conflict gets buried, not resolved.

The scent-first reintroduction

Once both cats are calm, do not just open the door and hope. Run the same staged plan used for a new arrival: keep them in separate zones, swap bedding so each smells the other before seeing it, feed on opposite sides of a closed door and move the bowls closer over days, then allow sight contact through a gap or gate before any free-roaming contact. Appetite is your progress gate — a cat who eats well in view of the other is coping; a cat who skips the meal needs the previous stage repeated. The full sequence is in how to introduce cats.

When to get help, and when it is not a behaviour problem

Escalate to a vet rather than a trainer first: sudden aggression in an older cat is often pain — arthritis, dental disease — or hyperthyroidism, and an irritable cat is sometimes an hurting cat. Fights that break skin need wound care; abscesses are the classic aftermath of cat fights and show up days later as a hot, painful swelling.

If medical causes are excluded and reintroduction stalls, a qualified feline behaviourist is the right next step. Persistent conflict with injuries is a welfare problem for both cats, not something to outlast.
