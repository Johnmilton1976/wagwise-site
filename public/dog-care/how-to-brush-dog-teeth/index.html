How to Brush a Dog's Teeth — A Step-by-Step Method

Daily brushing is the only proven way to prevent periodontal disease in dogs. Done in stages, most dogs accept it within three weeks.

Why this matters more than most owners think

By around three years of age, the majority of dogs have some degree of periodontal disease. It is the most common disease in dogs, and it is almost entirely preventable with one daily habit.

The consequences are not cosmetic. Periodontal bacteria enter the bloodstream through inflamed gums and are associated with changes in the heart, kidneys and liver. A dog with painful gums often eats less, loses weight, and becomes withdrawn in ways owners attribute to ageing. Dental pain in dogs is chronically under-recognised because dogs do not stop eating until it is severe.

The good news: brushing works, and a dog can be trained to accept it at almost any age.

Do not use bicarbonate of soda as a substitute paste. It is abrasive on enamel, and in quantity it disturbs the dog's acid-base balance. Dog toothpaste is cheap; human toothpaste is a poisoning risk.

The three-week training plan

Most brushing failures are not technique problems — they happen because the owner went from zero to a full brush in one session. Build it in stages, and stop each session while the dog is still comfortable.

WeekWhat you doSession length
1Let the dog lick paste off your finger. Nothing else. Praise, finish.10–20 seconds
2Rub paste onto the outer surface of the canine teeth with a finger, then a finger brush20–30 seconds
3Introduce the brush, one or two strokes per side, then build up30–60 seconds
Week 4+Full brush, all outer surfaces1–2 minutes

Do this at a calm time, not when the dog is excited about a walk. Many owners find it easiest after the evening meal, when the dog is settled. Keep sessions short and end on success — a two-second session that goes well is worth more than a two-minute session that ends in a struggle.

The technique

Angle. Hold the brush at about 45 degrees to the tooth, pointing toward the gum line. That is where plaque accumulates and where periodontal disease starts.

Motion. Small circles along the gum line, then a sweep downward toward the tip of the tooth. Work in sections: back molars on one side, then the canines, then the incisors at the front.

Focus on the outer surfaces. That is where plaque forms; the inner surfaces are cleaned largely by the tongue and are hard to reach without a fight. Brushing the outside well beats a stressful attempt at both.

Do not force the mouth open. Lift the lip with one hand while supporting the head with the other. A dog that has its mouth held open will struggle; a dog whose lip is lifted usually will not.

If the gums bleed during brushing, do not stop — mild bleeding in an early-gum-disease dog usually improves within a week or two of daily brushing, because the inflammation is what is bleeding. Bleeding that gets worse, or that comes with a bad smell, needs a vet.

How often is enough

Daily is the target: plaque mineralises into tartar within about 24–48 hours, and once it hardens, brushing cannot remove it. Every other day is the realistic minimum for meaningful protection. Once a week is mostly symbolic — better than nothing, but not disease-preventing.

If your routine can only support three sessions a week, do three sessions, and add a dental chew on the other days. Partial protection is worth having.

Look for the VOHC (Veterinary Oral Health Council) seal on chews and diets. It means the product has been tested against a standard, which most dental products have not.

Professional cleaning, and the anaesthesia question

Brushing prevents disease; it does not remove tartar that has already formed. A dog with visible tartar, inflamed gums or bad breath needs a professional scale and polish, and that requires general anaesthesia — the cleaning has to go under the gum line, which cannot be done on a conscious dog.

Avoid "anaesthesia-free dental cleaning". It is offered widely and it is a bad deal: only the visible crown surface can be scaled, the subgingival deposits that cause disease are untouched, and the process often damages the enamel. Worse, it creates the impression that the mouth has been treated, which delays the procedure that would actually help. The American Veterinary Dental College advises against it.

Warning signs that need a vet, not a brush

  • Persistent bad breath that returns quickly after brushing.
  • Gums that are red, swollen, or bleeding spontaneously.
  • Loose teeth, or a tooth that has changed colour.
  • Chewing on one side, dropping food, or refusing hard food.
  • Pawing at the mouth or rubbing the face on furniture.
  • Swelling under the eye — a tooth-root abscess can drain there.
  • Sudden refusal to eat, with drooling.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I brush my dog's teeth?
Daily is ideal — plaque hardens into tartar within 24–48 hours. Every other day is the realistic minimum for disease prevention. Once a week offers little protection.
Can I use human toothpaste on my dog?
No. Human toothpaste contains fluoride and often xylitol, both toxic to dogs, and the foaming agent makes them gag. Use dog-specific enzymatic paste.
What if my dog won't let me brush its teeth?
Build it in stages over three weeks: paste on a finger, then a finger brush, then a soft brush, keeping each session under 30 seconds at first. End on success rather than pushing through resistance.
Do dental chews replace brushing?
No, but VOHC-approved chews help between brushings. They are a supplement to brushing, not a replacement.
Why do my dog's gums bleed when I brush?
Mild bleeding from inflamed gums usually improves within a week or two of daily brushing. Bleeding that worsens, or comes with a bad smell, needs a vet.
Is anaesthesia-free teeth cleaning safe?
It is not recommended. It only cleans the visible crown, leaves the disease-causing deposits under the gum line, and can damage enamel while creating a false sense of security.
How do I know if my dog has dental disease?
Bad breath, red or bleeding gums, tartar on the teeth, chewing on one side, dropping food, or pawing at the mouth. Most dogs over three have some degree of it.
Can puppies have their teeth brushed?
Yes — start early with a finger and paste so the habit forms before the adult teeth arrive. Puppies lose their baby teeth around 4–6 months, so keep sessions gentle during that period.
