DOG CARE

Dog Care & Grooming at Home

Everyday dog care you can do at home: brushing teeth, trimming nails safely, bathing, and cleaning ears — all without turning it into a fight or a fuss.

What routine care actually looks like

Dogs do not complain about the things that are done to them weekly, which is exactly why routine care slips. The list is short, and almost all of it takes less time than a walk.

TaskHow oftenWhy it matters
Brushing teethDaily, or at least every other dayPlaque hardens into tartar within 48 hours; most dogs over three have some periodontal disease
Body check by handWeeklyLumps, sore spots and hot ears are found by touch long before they are found by eye
Ear checkWeekly for floppy-eared breedsA smell or dark discharge is the first sign of infection, and it is treatable at that stage
Brushing coatTwo to three times a weekPrevents matting and spreads skin oils; double coats need more in shedding season
Nail trimEvery three to four weeksOverlong nails change how the paw lands, which puts strain on the joints
BathEvery four to eight weeksMore often strips the coat's oils; less often is usually fine for a healthy dog
Weigh-inMonthlyWeight is the cheapest early warning for heart, thyroid and joint disease

The three things owners skip, and what they cost

Teeth. The single most skipped task and the one with the clearest consequence. By the time bad breath is obvious, there is usually tartar below the gumline, and treating it means an anaesthetic dental. Two minutes a day with a dog toothbrush is the whole intervention. If your dog has never had it done, start with a finger and toothpaste formulated for dogs — human toothpaste is not safe to swallow.

Nails. Dogs that walk on pavement still need trims, because the dewclaw never touches the ground and the side nails wear unevenly. A dog that clicks on hard floors is overdue. The risk of cutting too deep is real, so take small slices and have styptic powder to hand — the full method is in how to trim dog nails without the drama.

Ears. Floppy ears trap warmth and moisture, which is why spaniels, retrievers and poodles top the infection statistics. A weekly sniff and a wipe of the visible folds with a vet-approved cleaner is enough. Do not push cotton buds into the canal, and do not use alcohol or vinegar mixes on a dog that already has sore ears.

Care by life stage

StageWhat moves to the frontVet rhythm
Puppy (under 12 months)Handling practice, teeth habit, nails while they are still small enough to holdVaccination course, then monthly weigh-ins
Adult (1–7 years)Weight maintenance, dental care, coat and ear routineAnnual check, dental check every visit
Senior (7 years and up)Joint comfort, softer bedding, shorter and more frequent walksEvery six months, with bloodwork

The reason senior care is listed separately is that the routine changes shape rather than intensity. A senior dog does not need fewer checks — it needs them more often, and it needs the owner to notice slower stairs and reluctance to jump before it becomes a diagnosis.

When grooming turns into a vet visit

Stop and call the vet rather than continuing, if you see: bleeding that does not stop within a few minutes of pressure, a nail that keeps bleeding after styptic, gums that bleed when you brush, a head that is suddenly sensitive to being touched, a bad smell from one ear with head shaking, or a patch of skin that is red, weeping or spreading.

Two things are not emergencies but should be booked in: a dog that has started resisting having its paws handled (pain, not stubbornness, in most cases), and a dog that has developed a new lump you can feel but not see. Both are cheap to check and expensive to leave.
