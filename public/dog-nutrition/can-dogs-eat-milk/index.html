Can Dogs Drink Milk? The Numbers Behind the Usual No

Milk is not toxic, but it is the wrong food for a species that stopped making lactase at weaning — and the gap is measurable: cow's milk carries 44% of the energy of dog milk and 1.4 times its lactose.

How this page was put together — and what it is not

No dog was dosed with milk for this page, and nobody on the team is a veterinarian. What it rests on: published milk-composition data (Hand et al., Small Animal Clinical Nutrition, 5th ed.; Zhang et al., Foods 2022) and the veterinary references listed at the end. That is a real basis for a feeding guide, but it is not a substitute for a vet who knows your dog, and it is not individually reviewed by a veterinarian.

If your dog has a health condition — diabetes, pancreatitis, a known dairy reaction — treat this as background, not instructions. The numbers below tell you what is in the bowl; they do not tell you what your dog should eat.

Is milk good for dogs?

No, not as a drink. Milk is not toxic, and a lap of it will not poison a dog, but it is the wrong food for a species that stopped producing lactase at weaning. That is the whole answer, and the rest of this page is the arithmetic behind it — because "milk upsets dogs" is repeated everywhere and almost never quantified, which leaves owners guessing whether a splash in the bowl matters.

The honest version: most adult dogs handle a small amount without symptoms, a minority react to any dairy at all, and nobody can tell you which dog yours is without trying it. What can be measured is how much lactose, protein and energy is actually in the bowl, and how far that is from what a dog is built to digest.

What is actually in the bowl

Mother dog's milk is the reference point, because it is the only milk a dog's gut evolved to process. Set cow's milk and goat's milk beside it and the "just give them a little" advice starts to look like a compromise rather than a plan.

Composition of canine, feline, bovine and caprine milk, adapted from Small Animal Clinical Nutrition, 5th ed. (Hand, Thatcher, Remillard, Roudebush, 2010). Canine lactose measured at 1.56–3.92% across breeds in Zhang et al., Foods 2022;11(3):472.

Two numbers in that table do all the work. Cow's milk carries 44% of the energy of dog milk (64 vs 146 kcal) while carrying 1.4 times the lactose (4.7 vs 3.3 g). So a puppy fed cow's milk gets less than half the calories per millilitre and more of the one component it cannot digest. That is not a subtle imbalance. It is the mechanism behind "never use cow's milk for orphaned puppies", and it is why the failure shows up as diarrhea plus poor growth rather than one or the other.

Why does milk upset dogs?

Lactose is a disaccharide: one glucose molecule bonded to one galactose molecule. It cannot cross the gut wall in that form. Lactase, the enzyme that splits it, is abundant in nursing puppies and declines sharply after weaning — that decline is normal, not a defect, and it is the same thing that happens in most adult humans.

Undigested lactose then does two things at once, and it is worth separating them because they explain different symptoms. Gut bacteria ferment it, producing gas and cramping. And the intact sugar stays in the intestine as an osmotically active particle, holding water with it — that is the loose stool. A dog that gets gas but normal stools is somewhere in the middle of the spectrum; a dog with sudden watery diarrhea after dairy is at the far end.

How much lactose is in a normal portion

Veterinary literature does not give a lactose threshold for dogs the way it does for people, because tolerance varies too much between individuals to name a number. What you can do is count grams, so you know whether you are offering a taste or a dose.

PortionLactoseRoughly equivalent to
1 tablespoon of milk (15 ml)0.7 ga taste

That table explains the otherwise odd fact that cheese is usually fine and milk usually is not. Both are dairy; the difference is where the lactose goes. In hard cheese nearly all of it leaves with the whey, so per 100 g cheddar carries roughly 0.1–0.5 g against 4.7 g in milk — an order of magnitude, not a nuance. A 30 g cube therefore delivers less lactose than a single tablespoon of milk, while delivering more fat and salt than most owners expect.

How much milk can a dog have?

There is no amount that is good for a dog, so the practical rule is about symptoms rather than volume:

  • Under 1 g of lactose — a tablespoon or two — is tolerated by most dogs. This is the range where milk works as a taste, not a drink.
  • Around 5 g (100 ml) is where dogs with partial lactase production start producing gas. Many are fine; some are not.
  • Above 10 g (a full cup) produces loose stool in a large share of adult dogs, and reliably in dogs that have reacted before.
  • Zero is correct for any dog that has already had diarrhea or gas after dairy. There is nothing in milk a dog cannot get from a complete diet, so there is no reason to retry it.

One caution about using this as a tolerance test: a dog's reaction is not a reliable allergy test. Lactose intolerance is a digestive limit and shows up as gut signs. A true milk allergy is an immune reaction to milk protein and shows up as itching, hives or facial swelling — and it can appear with a dose far smaller than the amounts above. If you see skin signs rather than stomach signs, stop and treat it as an allergy, not a tolerance question.

"Lactose-free" milk: what it fixes, and what it doesn't

This is the part almost every "can dogs drink milk" page gets wrong. Lactose-free milk is ordinary milk treated with lactase, so the lactose is already split into glucose and galactose before it reaches the bowl. It is not lower in sugar. A cup of it carries the same 11.3 g of sugars as a cup of regular milk; only the form has changed.

That matters for dogs more than the label suggests, because of osmolarity. One lactose molecule is one particle. Split it, and you get two. Do the arithmetic on a cup: 11.3 g of lactose is about 33 millimoles of particles; the same 11.3 g broken into glucose and galactose is about 63 millimoles. The sugar load is identical and the particle count has nearly doubled — which is the side of the equation that pulls water into the gut.

So lactose-free milk solves the enzyme problem and leaves the osmotic one. For a dog with almost no lactase it is genuinely the better of the two, because nothing has to be digested. For a dog that produces some lactase, a large bowl of it can still produce soft stool, and the owner concludes the dog "reacted to lactose-free milk" when what happened was volume. Small amounts, same as before.

"Lactose-free" says nothing about what else is in the carton. Flavoured lactose-free milks — chocolate, strawberry, vanilla — are the ones to watch: chocolate is toxic to dogs, and sugar-free versions can contain xylitol, which is far more dangerous than any amount of lactose. Plain, unflavoured, and nothing else in the ingredients.

Goat milk is not the low-lactose option it is sold as

Goat milk has a reputation as the gentle dairy, and pet shops sell it as a near-substitute for mother's milk. The table above shows what it actually is: 4.0 g of lactose per 100 ml against 4.7 in cow's milk. That is 15% less, not "low lactose", and it is still well above the 3.3 g in dog milk. Its energy density is 69 kcal per 100 ml — less than half of dog milk's 146.

Where goat milk does differ is in the fat globules, which are smaller and form a softer curd, so some dogs seem to tolerate it slightly better in practice. That is a real difference in digestion, but it is a difference in texture, not in lactose. A dog that reacts to cow's milk with gas will usually react to goat's milk the same way at the same volume.

Fermented goat milk products — kefir, and yogurt made from goat milk — are a different case again, because the cultures have already consumed part of the lactose. They still are not a milk replacement, and they still should not be given in bowl-sized portions.

Is milk safe for puppies?

No, and this is the one place where the answer is absolute rather than a matter of volume. An orphaned or supplementary-fed puppy needs a purpose-made bitch milk replacer, not dairy milk from the carton. The numbers from the table above explain why: cow's milk supplies 44% of the energy and 44% of the protein of dog milk, half the calcium, and 1.4 times the lactose, all at once.

A puppy raised on cow's milk is being under-fed and over-challenged simultaneously, and the visible result — loose stool and slow growth — is easy to misread as "a sensitive puppy" rather than "the wrong food". Replacer formulas exist precisely because matching canine milk takes 7.5 g of protein and 9.5 g of fat per 100 ml, which no supermarket dairy does.

Cow's milk is not a dilute version of mother's milk. It is a different food: less than half the energy, half the protein, and more lactose. Use a bitch milk replacer and follow its feeding chart by body weight.

Better dairy options: yogurt and cheese

If you want to share dairy, the two options that make sense are the two with the least lactose:

  • Plain, unsweetened yogurt — around 4–5 g lactose per 100 g, and part of that has already been fermented by the live cultures, which is why it usually sits better than the same volume of milk. A spoonful or two is a treat, not a supplement.
  • Hard cheese in small cubes — under 0.5 g lactose per 30 g. The limit here is calories and salt, not lactose, so keep it to a cube or two.
  • Avoid flavoured, sweetened or chocolate dairy — the sugar and the chocolate are a bigger problem than the milk, and "dental" or "senior" yogurts can carry sweeteners including xylitol.

Signs your dog doesn't tolerate milk

Watch for loose stools, excess gas, stomach gurgling, or mild cramping within a few hours of drinking milk. If these appear, drop dairy and offer water instead. Persistent diarrhea deserves a vet check.

Timing is the useful clue. Lactose symptoms arrive within roughly 30 minutes to a few hours and resolve as the sugar clears the gut. Vomiting that starts immediately, or that repeats, is a different problem and should not be filed under "dairy sensitivity" — our dog vomiting guide sets out what is urgent.

What about almond, oat, or soy milk?

These plant milks are not toxic, but they are not a good idea either. Almond milk is low in protein and often sweetened; oat and soy milks can contain added sugars or even xylitol, which is toxic to dogs, so always read the label. None of them offer nutrition a dog actually needs, and plain water remains the better choice. A tiny taste of an unsweetened, xylitol-free milk alternative is unlikely to harm, but there is no reason to make it a habit.

One thing worth checking on any plant milk: "unsweetened" and "xylitol-free" are different claims. A carton can be unsweetened and still contain a sugar alcohol for body, and xylitol is the one that sends dogs to hospital. If the ingredient list is not in front of you, do not pour it. Our toxic foods list covers the sweeteners that matter.

If your dog drank milk — what to do

Most milk incidents are mild and need no vet. Use this timeline rather than guessing:

Time since drinkingWhat to watch forWhat to do
0–2 hoursUsually nothing yet; lactose is still in the gutOffer water. Do not induce vomiting. Note how much and what kind of milk.
2–8 hoursGas, gurgling, loose stool — the usual lactose signsWithhold further dairy. Most dogs pass it within a day; watch hydration.
8–24 hoursPersistent diarrhea, lethargy, not eatingCall a vet if it lasts beyond a day or the dog seems unwell.
Any timeVomiting that repeats, hives, facial swelling, difficulty breathingThese are allergy or choking signs, not lactose — call a vet now.

One rule settles most cases: a one-off lap is a non-event for the majority of adult dogs. The dogs that need help are the ones with repeated diarrhea, a known dairy reaction, or skin and breathing signs rather than gut signs.

Bottom line

Milk is not poison, but it is also not a treat a dog needs: it delivers the one sugar adult dogs are worst equipped to handle, at less than half the energy density of the milk they evolved on. A tablespoon in the bowl is harmless for most dogs and pointless for all of them. Milk-free water, a cube of cheese, or a spoonful of plain yogurt all do the same job better. For a fruit treat that is genuinely low-risk, banana is the safer habit to build.

Frequently asked questions

Is milk good for dogs?
Usually not. Most adult dogs are lactose intolerant, so milk tends to cause gas and diarrhea rather than provide benefit. Water is the best drink.
Why does milk give dogs diarrhea?
Most adult dogs are lactose intolerant — they lose lactase after weaning, so milk sugar ferments in the gut and loosens stools.
Can dogs have yogurt instead of milk?
Plain, unsweetened yogurt is often better tolerated than milk and adds probiotics. Keep it to a spoonful.
Can puppies drink cow's milk?
No — use a proper puppy milk replacer. Cow's milk is unbalanced for puppies and can cause diarrhea and poor growth.
How much milk can a dog drink?
Under 1 g of lactose — a tablespoon or two — is tolerated by most dogs. 100 ml (4.7 g) gives gas in dogs with partial lactase; a full cup (11.3 g) loosens stools in many adults. Any dog that has reacted before should get none.
Is cheese okay for dogs?
Small amounts of plain cheese are usually fine and many dogs tolerate it, but it is calorie-dense, so keep it occasional.
What should dogs drink instead of milk?
Water. Every dog should drink water; milk is not a necessary or healthy regular drink.
My dog drank milk and seems fine — is it okay to keep giving it?
Some dogs tolerate small amounts. If there are no digestive signs, an occasional sip is fine, but it should never replace water.
Is lactose-free milk safe for dogs?
Safer than regular milk for a dog with little lactase, because the sugar is already split and needs no digestion — but it is not lower in sugar. A cup still carries about 11.3 g of sugars, now as glucose and galactose, which nearly doubles the particle count and can still pull water into the gut. Small amounts only, and check that nothing else was added.
Is goat milk better than cow's milk for dogs?
Only slightly. Goat milk has 4.0 g of lactose per 100 ml against 4.7 in cow's milk — 15% less, not low-lactose, and still above the 3.3 g in dog milk. Its fat forms a softer curd, which some dogs tolerate better, but the lactose load is nearly the same.
