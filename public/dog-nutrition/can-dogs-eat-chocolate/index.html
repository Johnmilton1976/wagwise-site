Can Dogs Eat Chocolate? NO — It Is Toxic

No — chocolate is toxic to dogs because it contains theobromine, which they cannot metabolize well. Dark and baking chocolate are the most dangerous.

Is chocolate bad for dogs?

Yes. Chocolate is toxic to dogs because it contains theobromine (and caffeine), stimulants their bodies break down far more slowly than ours. The theobromine builds up and over-excites the heart and nervous system. This is why "is chocolate bad for dogs" gets an unqualified yes — and why the type and amount matter so much.

Why is chocolate toxic to dogs?

Humans metabolise theobromine quickly; dogs do not — it can linger in their system for 17–24 hours or more, so even a moderate amount keeps stimulating them the whole time. The darker and purer the chocolate, the more theobromine it carries, which is why baking and dark chocolate are far more dangerous than milk chocolate.

How much chocolate is dangerous?

The danger depends on two things: the type of chocolate and your dog's weight. Theobromine content varies roughly like this:

Type of chocolateTheobromine per ounce (approx.)Risk level
White chocolate~0.25 mgNegligible theobromine (but still fat and sugar)
Milk chocolate~44–60 mgMild risk in small dogs
Dark / semi-sweet (50–60% bars, chips)~150–160 mgDangerous in small amounts
Baking / unsweetened, 85%+ bars~390–450 mgVery dangerous in tiny amounts
Cocoa powder (dry)~737–800 mgExtremely dangerous

Bars labelled 70% or higher do not have their own row because cocoa-solids content varies too much between brands; they sit between the dark and the baking rows, so treat an unknown dark bar as baking chocolate. The full arithmetic, per kilogram of dog, is in the chocolate toxicity calculator — it takes the weight, the chocolate type and the amount, and returns the mg/kg figure a vet will ask for.

As a rough guide, mild signs begin around 20 mg of theobromine per kg of body weight, and severe or life-threatening signs around 40–50 mg/kg. For a 10 kg (22 lb) dog, that is mild effects from roughly 3–4 oz of milk chocolate but under half an ounce of baking chocolate; for a 25 kg dog, about 9 oz of milk chocolate or just over 1 oz of baking chocolate.

When in doubt, assume it is serious. Small dogs, dark chocolate, and any baking chocolate or cocoa powder all move quickly into dangerous territory.

Three anchors make the table usable in a kitchen at midnight. A standard milk chocolate bar is about 45 g, so a 5 kg dog is a bar away from the first signs and two and a half bars from the cardiac range. A single square of unsweetened baking chocolate is about 28 g, which takes a 10 kg dog straight past the first-signs line (13 g) and to the edge of the cardiac range (27 g) — a dog that has eaten one square is a phone call, not a wait-and-see. And cocoa powder is the outlier: at roughly 27 mg of theobromine per gram it needs no arithmetic at all — a heaped tablespoon in a cake mix is a veterinary call for any dog under 15 kg.

White chocolate is the exception that proves the rule. It has almost no theobromine, so the table above does not apply: a 5 kg dog would have to eat close to two kilos of it before the stimulant mattered, and by then the fat and sugar have caused pancreatitis instead. The dose is not the reason to keep white chocolate away from a dog — the 30 g of fat per 100 g is.

Where the table stops being useful is cocoa percentages between 60% and 85%, which is most of the dark bar shelf. Take the worst case: treat a 70–85% bar as baking chocolate and you will over-estimate the risk, which is the correct direction to be wrong in when the alternative is waiting for symptoms.

What are the symptoms of chocolate poisoning?

Signs usually appear within a few hours and can escalate. Watch for:

  • Restlessness, panting, and a racing heart.
  • Vomiting and diarrhea.
  • Tremors, muscle twitching, and in severe cases seizures.
  • High body temperature and, without treatment, collapse.

How long after eating chocolate do symptoms start?

Theobromine is absorbed within a few hours and then clears very slowly, so what matters is not only the peak but how long the dog stays above the threshold. A timeline, from the case literature:

Time after eatingWhat to expect
0–2 hoursNothing visible. This is the only window in which induced vomiting is useful — which is why the phone call should not wait for symptoms
4–6 hoursFirst signs in a significant dose: restlessness, panting, thirst, vomiting, a raised heart rate
6–12 hoursThe usual peak. Tremors, arrhythmia and, at high doses, seizures tend to appear here
12–72 hoursSlow decline. The half-life is about 17.5 hours in dogs, so a dog can stay symptomatic for two or three days without treatment

Two practical consequences. A dog that seems fine at three hours has not cleared the risk, so do not cancel the vet call on that basis. And a dog that is still trembling at 24 hours is expected to be — it is not a sign that treatment failed, it is the half-life doing what the half-life does. The dose bands behind this timeline are worked out per kilogram in the chocolate toxicity calculator.

What to do if your dog eats chocolate

Call your veterinarian or an animal poison control line right away. Note the type of chocolate, roughly how much, and your dog's weight — those three facts decide how serious it is.
  • Small amounts of milk chocolate may cause only mild upset in a large dog, but any dark, semisweet, or baking chocolate is urgent.
  • Keep all chocolate, cocoa powder, and baked goods well out of reach.
  • Do not try to make your dog vomit at home — let a professional assess and act.

What will the emergency vet do?

  • Decontamination: If the chocolate was eaten recently and your dog is stable, the vet may induce vomiting and give activated charcoal.
  • Supportive care: IV fluids and medications to control heart rate, tremors, and seizures as needed.
  • Monitoring: Because theobromine lingers, the vet may keep your dog under observation for many hours.

Chocolate vs other toxic foods

Chocolate is one of several foods that do not belong near dogs. Grapes and raisins are a different but equally serious risk — see our guides to grapes and our full toxic foods list. If your dog is already vomiting, our dog vomiting guide explains what is urgent.

Frequently asked questions

Is chocolate bad for dogs?
Yes. Chocolate contains theobromine, which dogs metabolise slowly, so it builds up and over-stimulates the heart and nervous system.
How much chocolate is toxic to a dog?
It depends on the type and your dog's weight. Mild signs start around 20 mg of theobromine per kg of body weight; severe signs around 40–50 mg/kg. Dark, semisweet, baking, and cocoa powder are dangerous in small amounts.
Is one bar of chocolate dangerous for a dog?
It depends on the bar and the dog. A standard 45 g milk chocolate bar sits just under the first-signs line for a 5 kg dog, but two and a half of them reach the cardiac range. The same 45 g of baking chocolate would put a 10 kg dog past the cardiac threshold. Read the cocoa percentage before the weight.
Is dark chocolate worse for dogs than milk chocolate?
Yes — dark and baking chocolate carry far more theobromine per ounce than milk chocolate and are the most toxic.
What are the symptoms of chocolate poisoning in dogs?
Restlessness, panting, a racing heart, vomiting, diarrhea, tremors, and in severe cases seizures. Signs can appear within a few hours.
What should I do if my dog ate chocolate?
Call your vet or animal poison control with the type, amount, and your dog's weight as soon as possible. Do not try to induce vomiting at home.
How long do chocolate poisoning symptoms last in dogs?
Theobromine lingers in a dog's system for 17–24 hours or more, so the vet may monitor your dog for many hours even after early treatment.
Can a little milk chocolate hurt a large dog?
Often it causes only mild upset, but any amount of dark, semisweet, or baking chocolate is urgent, and small dogs are at risk even from milk chocolate.
Is white chocolate safe for dogs?
It has almost no theobromine, but it is still high in fat and sugar and is not a treat we recommend giving dogs.
