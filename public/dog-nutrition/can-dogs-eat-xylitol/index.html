Can Dogs Eat Xylitol? No — It Can Be Deadly Fast

No — xylitol (birch sugar) is one of the deadliest common ingredients for dogs. A tiny amount triggers a rapid insulin surge, crash blood sugar, and possible liver failure within hours. Check every label.

Short answer: absolutely not. Xylitol — also labeled "birch sugar" or "birch sap" — is an artificial sweetener that is harmless to humans but one of the most dangerous substances a dog can swallow. It does not take much: a few pieces of sugar-free gum can put a small dog in crisis within an hour.

Why xylitol is so dangerous

In humans, xylitol is absorbed slowly and barely affects insulin. In dogs, the body mistakes it for real sugar and releases a massive insulin surge. The result happens in two stages:

  • Stage 1 — hypoglycemia (low blood sugar), within 30–60 minutes: vomiting, weakness, wobbling, collapse, tremors, seizures. This stage can be fatal on its own.
  • Stage 2 — liver failure, which can develop 12–72 hours after larger doses, and can be fatal even with intensive care.

A concerning dose can be around 50 mg of xylitol per kg of body weight for blood-sugar crash, and roughly 10 times that for liver injury. One piece of many sugar-free gums contains 300–1,000 mg — so one or two pieces can seriously harm a small dog.

Where xylitol hides — check these labels

Xylitol appears in more products every year. Scan ingredient lists on: sugar-free gum and mints, candy, breath strips, sugar-free peanut and other nut butters, sugar-free desserts and protein bars, chewable and gummy vitamins, mouthwash and toothpaste, some liquid medications, and products labeled "birch sugar."

Peanut butter deserves special attention: some brands add xylitol. Always check before sharing — plain, xylitol-free peanut butter is the only kind dogs may have, in small amounts.

What to do if your dog ate xylitol

  1. Treat it as an emergency — this is not a wait-and-see situation. Contact a veterinarian or pet poison hotline immediately, day or night.
  2. Bring the product label — the exact product (gum, butter, candy) and the xylitol grams per serving let the vet calculate the risk.
  3. Do not wait for symptoms — treatment before the blood sugar crashes is dramatically more effective.
  4. Do not induce vomiting at home unless a professional directs you to; a collapsing dog should go straight to the clinic.
  5. Watch for 72 hours if the vet advises home monitoring: lethargy, vomiting, or yellow gums in the following days can signal liver injury.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

What is xylitol and why is it deadly for dogs?
It is a sugar alcohol sweetener, sometimes labeled birch sugar. In dogs it triggers a massive insulin release, crashing blood sugar within 30–60 minutes, and larger doses can cause fatal liver failure.
How much xylitol is toxic to dogs?
Blood-sugar crash can occur around 50 mg per kg of body weight; liver injury at roughly ten times that. One or two pieces of sugar-free gum can seriously harm a small dog.
Is xylitol in peanut butter?
Some brands add it — always check the ingredient list before sharing peanut butter. Look for xylitol, birch sugar, or birch sap on the label.
What are the first signs of xylitol poisoning?
Vomiting, sudden weakness, wobbling, collapse, tremors, and seizures — usually within an hour of eating it. Liver injury can follow days later.
My dog ate sugar-free gum — what should I do?
Treat it as an emergency. Call a vet or pet poison hotline immediately with the brand and how many pieces — do not wait for symptoms.
Can dogs recover from xylitol poisoning?
Yes, if treated early. Dogs treated before or during the blood-sugar crash often recover with glucose support; liver failure cases have a much worse outlook, which is why speed matters.
Is xylitol bad for cats too?
Cats appear far less sensitive and rarely eat it, but keep all sweetened products away from every pet anyway.
Is erythritol or stevia safe for dogs?
Erythritol and stevia are not known to cause xylitol-type poisoning, but sugar alcohols can cause diarrhea. The safest policy: no sweetened products for dogs at all.
