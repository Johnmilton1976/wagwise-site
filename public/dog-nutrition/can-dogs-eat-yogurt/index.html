Can Dogs Eat Yogurt? Yes — Plain and Unsweetened

Yes — plain, unsweetened yogurt is a safe, gut-friendly treat for many dogs, thanks to lower lactose than milk and live cultures. The catches are lactose intolerance and, above all, any yogurt sweetened with xylitol.

Is yogurt safe for dogs?

Plain, unsweetened yogurt is not toxic to dogs and is a reasonable occasional treat. Because the live cultures break down much of the lactose, yogurt is easier on the gut than plain milk, and it brings a little protein and calcium plus probiotics. But many dogs are still at least mildly lactose intolerant, so start with a small amount and watch the results.

How to choose the right yogurt

  • Plain: no added sugar, no fruit, no flavoring.
  • Unsweetened: never any product with xylitol or other artificial sweeteners — xylitol is highly toxic to dogs.
  • Greek is best: strained yogurt is thicker and even lower in lactose, so it suits more dogs.
  • Avoid: flavored, 'light', or diet yogurts — they are the ones most likely to hide xylitol or sugar alcohols.
The single most important rule: the yogurt must contain no xylitol. Check the label every time, because a 'sugar-free' yogurt can be deadly for a dog.

Yogurt nutrition at a glance

For scale, here is roughly what is in 100 g of plain low-fat yogurt:

NutrientPer 100 g
Calories~59 kcal
Protein~10 g
Fat~1.5 g
Sugar (natural)~3–4 g
Calcium~110 mg

Modest protein and calcium with low natural sugar make plain yogurt a decent occasional topper — once you rule out sweetener and lactose issues.

How much yogurt is safe?

Treats of any kind should stay under about 10% of a dog's daily calories. A sensible occasional amount by body weight:

Dog weightSafe occasional amount
Under 5 kg (toy / small)1 teaspoon
5–15 kg1–2 tablespoons
15–30 kg2–4 tablespoons
30 kg and upUp to 1/4 cup, occasionally

How to serve yogurt

  • Offer a plain spoonful, or freeze dollops for a cool treat.
  • Use it to hide a pill for a fussy taker (vet approval first).
  • Stick to plain — no granola, no honey drizzle, no fruit mix-ins.

Are there risks?

The two real catches: xylitol-sweetened yogurt is an emergency, and lactose-intolerant dogs may get gas or diarrhea. If your dog reacts badly, stop and switch to a non-dairy option. For the full list of dangerous additions, see our toxic foods guide. Note that milk is higher in lactose, so yogurt is usually the gentler dairy choice.

Can puppies eat yogurt?

A small amount of plain yogurt is not toxic to puppies, but their guts are sensitive, so keep portions tiny and watch for loose stools. Plain pumpkin or a vet-recommended puppy food is a safer everyday choice.

When yogurt isn't the answer

Yogurt is a fun occasional topper, not a probiotic cure. If your dog has ongoing digestive trouble, our feeding guide and a vet visit beat a yogurt tub. Persistent diarrhea, vomiting, or bloating needs a professional, not dairy.

Frequently asked questions

Can dogs eat plain yogurt?
Yes — plain, unsweetened yogurt is a safe occasional treat for many dogs and is easier to digest than milk thanks to lower lactose and live cultures.
Is Greek yogurt better for dogs?
Greek yogurt is strained and even lower in lactose, so it suits more dogs; just keep it plain and unsweetened.
Why must yogurt have no xylitol?
Xylitol is highly toxic to dogs and can cause a fatal blood-sugar crash; always check the label and avoid any 'sugar-free' yogurt.
Can lactose-intolerant dogs eat yogurt?
Many can tolerate small amounts because cultures lower the lactose, but some still get gas or diarrhea — start tiny and watch.
How much yogurt can a dog have?
Keep it under 10% of daily calories: a teaspoon for toy dogs, up to 1/4 cup for large dogs, only occasionally.
Can dogs eat flavored or fruit yogurt?
No — flavored and 'light' yogurts often hide sugar or xylitol; stick to plain, unsweetened yogurt only.
Can puppies eat yogurt?
A small amount of plain yogurt is not toxic, but puppy guts are sensitive, so keep portions tiny and watch for loose stools.
Is yogurt a probiotic cure for dogs?
No — it is a fun occasional topper, not a treatment; ongoing gut issues need a vet, not a yogurt tub.
