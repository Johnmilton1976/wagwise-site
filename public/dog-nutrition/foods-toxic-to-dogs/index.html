Foods Toxic to Dogs — The List Every Owner Needs

The most dangerous are chocolate, grapes and raisins, onions and garlic, xylitol (in sugar-free gum), and alcohol. Even small amounts can be serious for a small dog.

The four tiers, in one table

Most "toxic food" lists are a flat alphabetical dump, which is not how an owner needs the information at 10pm. What matters is how fast you have to act. These four tiers sort the same foods by the decision they trigger, and every item links to a full write-up with doses and symptoms.

Tier 1 in detail — the ones that actually kill

Chocolate. The toxin is theobromine and the dose is everything. Roughly: white chocolate 0.25 mg per gram, milk chocolate 2.4, dark chocolate 5 to 6, unsweetened baking chocolate 16 to 26, cocoa powder around 26 mg per gram. Signs start at about 20 mg per kg of body weight, heart rhythm problems appear around 40 to 50, and seizures above 60. A 10 kg dog eating 100 g of dark chocolate takes in roughly 550 mg — 55 mg per kg, squarely in the cardiac range. The same weight of milk chocolate lands at 24 mg per kg: upset stomach territory, not emergency territory. That gap is why "how much chocolate" has no single answer, and why the calculator matters more than the rule.

Grapes and raisins. No known safe dose. The mechanism is still unidentified, and individual dogs vary enormously — some eat a handful with no effect, others go into kidney failure from a few. Because the sensitivity cannot be predicted, any ingestion is treated as serious. Raisins are worse by weight, since drying concentrates whatever the toxin is.

Xylitol. A sugar alcohol in sugar-free gum, some mints, some peanut butters and an increasing number of "no added sugar" products. About 0.1 g per kg causes dangerous hypoglycaemia within 30 minutes; around 0.5 g per kg brings liver injury into play. For a 10 kg dog, that first threshold is one gram — less than a single stick of gum.

Onions and garlic. Both damage red blood cells; garlic is the more concentrated of the two. A single large ingestion causes haemolytic anaemia, but repeated small amounts in leftovers do the same thing slowly, which is how it is usually missed. Signs can take days to appear — pale gums, weakness, dark urine.

Alcohol and raw dough. Ethanol is ethanol whatever the source. Bread dough is a double problem: it rises in the warm stomach and distends it, and it ferments into alcohol while it sits there. A dog that ate dough is both bloated and drunk.

The eight symptoms that mean poisoning

Poisoning does not announce itself with one clean sign. The pattern to watch for is a dog that was normal an hour ago and is now some combination of: vomiting, drooling more than usual, tremors or twitching, wobbling or unable to stand, panting at rest, a racing or irregular heartbeat, gums that are pale or brick-red, and collapse. Seizures and bloody diarrhoea are late signs, not early ones.

Two behavioural signals are easy to dismiss and worth taking seriously: a dog that has suddenly gone very quiet and hidden, and a dog that is pressing its head against a wall or the back of the sofa. Both suggest something neurological rather than a stomach upset.

The first ten minutes

1. Remove access. Move the dog away from the source, then secure the packaging. You will need it.

2. Work out what and how much. The three numbers every vet or poison line will ask for: what was eaten, how much, and the dog's weight. The wrapper gives you the first two, and the cocoa percentage if it is chocolate.

3. Call before you act. Your vet, the practice's emergency number, or a pet poison service. Describe the numbers and the time it happened.

4. Note the time. Everything in toxicology is time-dependent — what can be done at 30 minutes cannot be done at three hours.

Do not induce vomiting unless a vet tells you to. It is wrong for caustic substances, wrong for a dog that is already wobbly, and it carries a real risk of aspiration pneumonia. The old advice to force salt water or hydrogen peroxide down a dog's throat causes more harm than the chocolate does.

What not to do

Do not give milk. It does not dilute a toxin, and it adds a lactose problem to an existing one.

Do not wait for symptoms. With xylitol and grapes in particular, the window where treatment is simple closes before the dog looks unwell. A dog that seems fine at 20 minutes is the normal presentation, not evidence that nothing happened.

Do not "dilute" with a big meal. Food in the stomach can delay absorption, but it also makes induced vomiting harder and can complicate anaesthesia if the dog needs it.

Do not rely on the internet instead of a professional. Use a page like this one to decide how urgent it is, then call.

Not food, but in the same category

The most common pet poisonings are not food at all. Human medication — ibuprofen, paracetamol, antidepressants, ADHD medication — is the leading category, and a single tablet can be enough for a small dog. Then antifreeze, which is sweet and lethal in tiny volumes; rodenticide, which causes internal bleeding days later; slug pellets; nicotine and cannabis edibles; and lilies, which cause kidney failure in cats from pollen alone. Grapes and chocolate get the headlines, but the medicine cabinet and the garage do more damage.

Safe swaps that keep the begging quiet

A dog that is used to getting something from your plate does not need the plate. Plain cooked chicken, a carrot, a green bean, a slice of cucumber, or a piece of apple with the core removed all satisfy the same ritual. Keep a small box of dog-safe treats where the human snacks live, so the thing you reach for is already the right thing.

Keeping it from happening again

Most repeat incidents come from the same three places: a bag left within reach, a child sharing food, and a counter surfer. Move chocolate and raisins into a closed cupboard rather than a low shelf, keep gum and mints out of bags on the floor, and teach a reliable "leave it" — it is the one cue that pays for itself in this exact situation. If your dog is a counter surfer, nothing on the worktop is safe; treat the kitchen as a room with a lid.

Frequently asked questions

How much chocolate is toxic to a dog?
Around 20 mg of theobromine per kg of body weight brings the first signs, 40 to 50 mg/kg causes heart rhythm problems, and above 60 mg/kg seizures are possible. A 10 kg dog reaches the cardiac range at roughly 100 g of dark chocolate, but would need far more milk chocolate to get there.
Are grapes really that dangerous?
Yes. There is no known safe dose and sensitivity varies between dogs, so a handful of grapes or raisins can cause acute kidney failure in one dog and nothing in another. Any ingestion is treated as serious.
Is xylitol in peanut butter?
In some brands. It appears on labels as xylitol, birch sugar, birch bark sugar or wood sugar, and it is most common in reduced-fat and no-added-sugar products. Read the ingredient list every time, because recipes change.
How long after eating poison do symptoms show?
It depends on the substance. Xylitol can collapse a dog within 30 minutes; chocolate signs usually appear in 4 to 12 hours; onion and garlic damage shows up 2 to 5 days later; kidney failure from grapes can take 24 to 72 hours. A dog that looks fine early on has not necessarily escaped it.
Should I make my dog vomit?
Only if a vet or poison service tells you to. It is dangerous with caustic substances, unsafe in a dog that is already wobbly or unconscious, and carries a risk of aspiration pneumonia. Never use salt water or hydrogen peroxide on your own.
My dog ate a small amount of onion — is that an emergency?
A single small piece in a medium or large dog is unlikely to cause a crisis, but repeated small amounts in leftovers cause the same anaemia slowly. Call your vet with the amount and the dog's weight, and watch for pale gums and weakness over the following days.
What foods are safe to share with a dog?
Plain cooked chicken, carrot, green beans, cucumber, apple without the core, blueberries, watermelon without seeds, plain cooked rice, and plain unsweetened yoghurt in small amounts. All of them count toward the 10% of daily calories that treats are allowed.
Is peanut butter safe if it is organic?
Organic says nothing about xylitol. The risk is the sweetener, not the farming method, so read the ingredient list on organic jars exactly as you would on any other.
