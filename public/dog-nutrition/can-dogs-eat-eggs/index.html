Can Dogs Eat Eggs? Cooked, Plain, and Moderate

Yes, cooked eggs are a healthy, protein-rich addition to a dog's diet. Raw eggs are best avoided because of salmonella risk and a biotin concern.

Can dogs eat eggs? Yes — cooked, plain, in moderation

Eggs are one of the most complete protein sources available, and a dog can digest a cooked egg almost entirely. They are also cheap, which makes them the most practical food topper for a dog that has gone off its kibble.

The three conditions that keep it safe: cooked, plain, and counted. Each one has a real reason behind it, covered below.

What an egg actually gives a dog

A large egg is about 70 calories, 6 g of protein and 5 g of fat, plus choline, selenium, riboflavin, vitamin B12 and folate. The amino acid profile is close to ideal, which is why egg is used as the reference protein in nutrition science.

For a dog recovering from illness, a fussy senior, or a dog that needs to gain weight slowly, a cooked egg stirred into the bowl does more than most supplements costing ten times as much.

Eggs count as treats, so the 10% rule applies: everything outside the complete diet should total under a tenth of daily calories. A medium dog eating four eggs a week is already at roughly 280 calories of extras — that is most of the treat budget gone.

Why raw eggs are a poor choice

Two separate problems, often bundled together as one.

Salmonella and Campylobacter. Raw eggs can carry both. Dogs are more resistant to salmonella than people are, but not immune — and a dog that licks a raw egg off the floor then licks a child's face is a transmission route that has nothing to do with the dog getting sick.

Avidin. Raw egg white contains avidin, a protein that binds biotin (vitamin B7) tightly enough that it cannot be absorbed. One raw white occasionally is trivial. Feeding raw whites daily over weeks is the pattern that causes a deficiency, and the classic sign is dull, brittle coat and skin problems. Cooking denatures avidin, which is why a cooked egg carries none of this risk.

The raw-feeding argument that "wolves eat eggs" skips a detail: wild canids eat eggs rarely and inconsistently. Daily raw whites are a modern feeding pattern, not an ancestral one.

How to cook them for a dog

  • Boiled. The simplest option. Eight to ten minutes, cool, peel. No fat added, nothing to burn.
  • Scrambled. Plain, in a non-stick pan with no oil or butter, no salt, no milk. Stir constantly and cook through — runny scrambled egg is undercooked egg.
  • Poached. Fine, but the white must be fully set, not translucent.
  • Never fried in oil. A fried egg is a fat bomb, and repeated high-fat extras are how a dog ends up with pancreatitis.

No seasoning of any kind. Salt is a genuine toxicity risk at sufficient dose, and onion and garlic powder — both common in egg dishes — are worse. A plain egg tastes fine to a dog; the seasoning is entirely for you.

Eggshells: the calcium question

Ground eggshell is roughly 95% calcium carbonate and is a legitimate calcium source in home-prepared diets. But it is only appropriate when a vet or a board-certified nutritionist has formulated the diet and calculated how much calcium the bowl actually needs. Sprinkling shell on top of a complete commercial food adds calcium that is already balanced, which pushes the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio out of range.

If you feed a complete diet — and most people do — skip the shells. If you home-cook, the shell goes in only as part of a formulated recipe.

Eggs and pancreatitis

An egg is not a high-fat food on its own at 5 g of fat. The problem is what eggs are usually cooked in and served with: butter, oil, cheese, bacon. A dog with a history of pancreatitis can usually have a plain boiled egg; it should never have the Sunday breakfast plate.

If your dog has had pancreatitis, ask the vet before adding eggs at all, and never introduce them during a flare.

Egg allergy in dogs

Genuine egg allergy exists but is uncommon, and it is usually a reaction to a specific protein in the white. Signs are itching, ear inflammation, or digestive upset appearing within hours to a couple of days of eating egg. If you suspect it, stop the egg for three weeks and reintroduce deliberately — that is more informative than any test sold online.

When to skip eggs

  • Known egg sensitivity or a confirmed food allergy trial that excluded egg.
  • Active pancreatitis, or a pancreatitis history without vet clearance.
  • Any dog mid-elimination-diet — adding a new protein mid-trial ruins the trial.
  • Raw eggs, for any dog, when there are young children or immunocompromised people in the house.

Practical ways to use eggs

The bland diet. Plain boiled chicken and rice is the traditional post-upset stomach meal; a boiled egg chopped through it adds protein without adding fat.

Pill delivery. A quarter of a hard-boiled egg holds most tablets better than cheese does, with far less salt and fat.

Fussy eaters. A warm boiled egg mashed into kibble releases aroma, which is what actually drives a dog's appetite. Warm, not hot.

Freezer treats. Whisked egg, cooked in a mug in the microwave until set, cubed and frozen — a two-ingredient treat with no additives.

Frequently asked questions

Can dogs eat scrambled eggs?
Yes, if they are plain — no butter, oil, salt, milk or cheese — and cooked fully through. Runny scrambled egg is undercooked.
Why not raw eggs for dogs?
Raw egg white contains avidin, which blocks biotin absorption, and raw eggs can carry salmonella and campylobacter. Cooking removes both problems.
How many eggs can a dog eat?
Roughly 3–4 eggs a week for a medium dog, 1–2 for a toy breed, up to 6–7 for a giant breed. Eggs count toward the 10% treat budget.
Can dogs eat eggshells?
Ground eggshell is a usable calcium source, but only in a home-prepared diet formulated by a vet or nutritionist. On top of a complete commercial food it unbalances the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio.
Can dogs eat boiled eggs?
Yes — boiled is the safest form. Cool it and peel it, and give it plain with no salt.
Are eggs good for a dog's coat?
Cooked eggs supply protein, biotin and fatty acids that support coat condition — but only as part of an adequate diet. Raw whites do the opposite over time by blocking biotin.
Can dogs with pancreatitis eat eggs?
A plain boiled egg is usually acceptable, because the fat is in what eggs are cooked in, not the egg. Ask the vet first if there is a pancreatitis history.
Can puppies eat eggs?
Yes, cooked and in small amounts — a quarter to half an egg for a young puppy. Introduce one new food at a time so you can tell what caused any upset.
