Dog Food Safety Chart: 47 Foods in Four Risk Tiers

Most "can my dog eat this" charts give two answers — safe or toxic. That is not the question anyone actually has at nine in the evening with a dog watching them eat. The question is how much, and which part.

Why this chart is built differently

Three things separate this page from the wall charts and laminated posters. First, every row carries a number, not an adjective. "High in salt" tells you nothing; "1,000 mg of sodium per 100 g against a daily need of 200 mg" tells you whether tonight matters. Second, there are four tiers instead of two, because the largest group of foods — the ones that are perfectly fine except for one attached part — gets flattened into "toxic" by a two-tier chart, and people then panic over a piece of watermelon and shrug at a macadamia nut. Third, every row links to the page that shows its working: the dose arithmetic, the source, and the date it was last checked.

If you only read one paragraph, read the tier definitions and then jump to the food you are holding.

The four tiers

TierNameWhat it means
Tier 1EverydayPlain, cooked where relevant, and small enough to stay inside the treat budget. Nothing here needs a calculation beyond portion size.
Tier 2A ceilingSafe, but carries sugar, fat, lactose or a compound that stops being harmless at volume. These are the foods where the amount is the whole answer.
Tier 3One part is the problemThe flesh is fine and something attached to it is not — a pit, a cob, a skin, a green stage, a mould. This is the tier people misread most often.
Tier 4No safe doseEither no amount has been established as safe, or the amount that causes harm is small enough to arrive by accident. These end in a phone call, not a portion.

The 10% rule, in numbers

Every "safe" verdict on this page sits on top of one rule: treats of any kind should stay under about 10% of your dog's daily calories. It is worth doing the arithmetic once, because the answer surprises most owners.

A 10 kg dog on a typical maintenance diet eats roughly 600 kcal a day. Ten per cent of that is 60 kcal — and 60 kcal is:

  • about two and a half medium carrots, or
  • about three-quarters of a cup of blueberries, or
  • one large egg and a small piece of chicken, or
  • half a medium avocado, which would use the entire budget on 15% fat and no complete nutrition.

That is the whole reason a food can be "safe" and still be a bad idea. A dog does not need treats to be nutritious — it needs them to be small. When someone tells you their dog "only gets healthy treats" and the dog is overweight, this arithmetic is usually why.

This is the tier that causes the most unnecessary panic and the most unnecessary vet bills, in roughly equal measure. In every case the answer depends on which part the dog got, not on the food itself.

Tier 4 — No established safe dose

Do not wait for symptoms in this tier. The relevant instruction is a phone call, and the numbers below are what you read out to the vet.

FoodWhat is safeThe number that matters
AlcoholNoneEthanol depresses the central nervous system faster in dogs than in people, and it hides in rising dough and fallen fruit
CaffeineNoneMild signs from about 20 mg/kg, serious toxicity near 40–60 mg/kg, roughly 140 mg/kg fatal
ChocolateNoneA 10 kg dog shows mild signs from 3–4 oz of milk chocolate but under half an ounce of baking chocolate
GrapesNone — grapes and raisins alikeNo safe amount has ever been established; a 5 kg dog and three grapes is the same danger zone as a 30 kg dog and a bunch
Macadamia NutsNoneSigns from about 1 g of nut per kg — a couple of nuts for a 10 kg dog
XylitolNoneAbout 50 mg/kg triggers the insulin crash; around 500 mg/kg risks liver injury
Raw DoughNoneLive yeast produces ethanol in a warm stomach, and the dough expands while it does

The three mistakes that cause most of the trouble

"A little bit can't hurt." In Tier 4, a little bit is the dose. Macadamia nuts produce tremors from about 1 g of nut per kilogram of body weight — for a 10 kg dog that is a couple of nuts, not a handful. Xylitol reaches the blood-sugar threshold at 50 mg/kg, which is less than a single piece of some sugar-free gums for a small dog. The phrase "a little bit" is calibrated for humans eating human food, and it does not transfer.

"It's natural, so it's gentle." Xylitol is a plant-derived sweetener sold in health-food aisles. Amygdalin is marketed online as "vitamin B17". Black walnuts are simply walnuts that fell off a tree. Natural describes where a compound came from, not what it does to a dog's liver. The most dangerous items on this chart are all natural.

"He's eaten it before and was fine." This one is true for grapes and false as a safety argument. Individual dogs vary enormously in how they handle grape ingestion — some have survived a handful with no effect, others have gone into kidney failure after a few. No safe amount has ever been established, which means a previous uneventful grape is evidence about one dog on one day, not a green light.

What this chart deliberately leaves out

Being clear about the edges is part of being trustworthy about the middle. This chart does not cover:

  • Raw and undercooked meat. The risk is bacterial (Salmonella, Listeria, E. coli) rather than toxic, it depends on the supply chain, and it is a separate argument from "is this food poisonous".
  • Medication, supplements and human vitamins. These follow completely different dose logic and deserve their own page, not a row here.
  • Your dog's own history. A dog with pancreatitis should not be eating anything in the high-fat column regardless of tier. A dog with a calcium oxalate stone history should skip spinach outright. The chart gives the general answer; your vet gives yours.
  • Breed-specific sensitivity. Some breeds — Akitas and Shibas among them — are markedly more sensitive to Allium damage than the average dog, which moves garlic and onion from "avoid" to "strictly avoid" for them.

If something on this chart already happened

Skip the rest of the page and call. Have three things ready, because the vet will ask for all of them: what the food was (including the brand and whether it was sugar-free), how much (the packet is better than an estimate), and your dog's weight. Those three answers decide the treatment, and "he ate some gum" cannot be converted into a dose by anyone.

Do not induce vomiting unless a vet tells you to. For caustic or sharp items it makes the damage worse, and for some toxins the timing has already passed.

Sources and how this chart is maintained

The dose figures here are drawn from veterinary toxicology references and the underlying case literature, and each linked page names its own sources. Where a number is contested — the threshold for garlic, the exact toxic principle in avocado — the linked page says so rather than picking the tidiest figure.

Every row is re-checked on a rolling six-month cycle, and immediately when a source changes. If a number here disagrees with a source you trust, write to contact@wagwise.org with the row and the source, and it gets fixed and re-dated. The editorial standards page explains how the guides are written and reviewed.
